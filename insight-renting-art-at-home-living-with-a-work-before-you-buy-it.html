Renting Art at Home: Living with a Work Before You Buy It

Choosing a work of art in a gallery is one thing. Living with it every day, in your own light and your own rooms, is another. Renting allows you to find out how a work behaves at home before you commit to owning it.

No. 37  ·  Buying  ·  4 minute read

WHY RENT RATHER THAN BUY

  • Certainty. Some works reveal more over months. Others lose their appeal once the first impression fades. Renting tests that.
  • Scale and setting. A work that looks right in a white gallery may be too large, too dark or too strong for a particular room.
  • Flexibility. People who move often, or who are refurbishing, can live with good art without a long commitment.
  • Spreading the cost. Rental payments are made in instalments rather than as a single sum.

Renting is not a way to avoid the decision forever. For most people, it is a way to reach a better decision.

HOW A RENTAL AGREEMENT WORKS

Rental arrangements vary across the market. The key terms to look for are:

  • The term, meaning how long you will have the work.
  • The fee, usually calculated as a percentage of the work's sale price, paid monthly, quarterly or annually.
  • Insurance, covering who insures the work while it is in your home and on what basis.
  • Swaps, covering whether you can exchange the work for another during the term.
  • Purchase credit, covering whether rental payments count towards buying the work and, if so, how much.
  • Delivery, installation and collection, covering who pays and who carries out the work.
  • Care and access, covering where the work may hang, and when the owner may inspect it.
  • Ending early, covering what happens if you want to return the work before the term ends.

THE RSAS RENTAL TERMS

RSAS offers rental terms of one, two or three years. The annual rental fee is ten per cent of the work's sale price, paid quarterly. RSAS insures the work while it is with you, and you may swap it for another work during the term. All rental income paid goes towards the purchase price, so if you decide to buy, nothing you have paid is lost.

For example, a work priced at £20,000 would carry an annual rental fee of £2,000, payable in four quarterly instalments of £500. If, after two years, you decide to buy it, the £4,000 already paid is deducted, leaving £16,000 to pay.

WHAT TO CHECK BEFORE THE WORK ARRIVES

  • Placement. Choose a wall away from direct sunlight, radiators, fireplaces, kitchens and bathrooms. No. 46 in this series covers hanging and lighting.
  • Fixings. Make sure the wall can take the weight and that the installer uses suitable fixings.
  • Condition. Agree a short condition note, with photographs, when the work is delivered. It protects both you and the owner if a question arises later.
  • Household risks. Young children, pets, candles and parties all present risks. Tell the owner if any part of your home presents particular hazards.

LIVING WITH THE WORK

Pay attention to how the work changes with the light through the day and across the seasons. Notice whether you still look at it after a few months. Ask friends who visit what they see. These are the questions that a short visit to a gallery cannot answer.

WHEN THE TERM ENDS

At the end of the term you have three choices: buy the work, return it, or rent something else. If you buy, the purchase is completed with the rental credit applied, and you receive the invoice, certificate and documents that come with any sale. If you return it, the owner collects it and checks its condition against the delivery note.

RENTING FOR BUSINESSES

Businesses rent art for many of the same reasons, with added considerations around display in shared spaces and accounting. No. 08 in this series covers renting for hotels, and No. 30 covers corporate collections.
