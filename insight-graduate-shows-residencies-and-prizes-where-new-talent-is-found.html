Graduate Shows, Residencies and Prizes: Where New Talent Is Found

Before an artist has a gallery or an auction record, there are still signals to follow. Graduate shows, residencies and prizes are where much of the art world first notices new talent, and where our own research often begins.

Emerging Artists  ·  4 minute read

Every established artist was once unknown. The early stages of a career leave fewer traces than later ones: no auction history, limited exhibitions and prices that have barely been tested. Yet those early stages are where the most interesting discoveries are made, and where collectors who arrive first can acquire work on the most favourable terms.

The challenge is knowing where to look and how to read what you find.

GRADUATE AND DEGREE SHOWS

Art school degree shows are one of the first public tests of a young artist's work. Each year, the leading schools exhibit their graduating students, and the major shows attract curators, gallerists, critics and collectors alongside family and friends.

In the United Kingdom, the postgraduate shows at institutions such as the Royal College of Art, the Slade School of Fine Art, Goldsmiths, the Glasgow School of Art and the Royal Academy Schools are watched closely. Many artists now represented by leading galleries first came to attention there.

Prices at degree shows are usually modest, reflecting artists at the very start of their careers. That makes them an accessible entry point for collectors. It also means the evidence is thin. A strong degree show is promising. It is not yet a career.

What we look for at this stage is seriousness and distinctiveness. Is the work clearly the artist's own, rather than an echo of a fashionable style? Is there an idea being developed with depth and consistency? Have tutors, visiting curators or galleries already shown interest?

RESIDENCIES

After leaving art school, many artists apply for residencies. These provide time, space and sometimes funding to make work, often alongside other artists and within an institution.

Residencies vary enormously, from small studio programmes to highly competitive international placements attached to museums and foundations. Selection for a respected residency is a meaningful signal, because the selectors are usually curators and artists with good judgement and a strong interest in identifying talent early.

Residencies also tend to produce new bodies of work and lasting professional connections, both of which can accelerate a career.

PRIZES AND OPEN EXHIBITIONS

Art prizes and open submission exhibitions offer another early signal. Some are open to any artist, while others are restricted to recent graduates or artists at particular career stages.

The value of a prize depends on its judges and its history. A prize judged by respected curators, with a record of recognising artists who went on to significant careers, carries real weight. Being shortlisted for such a prize, even without winning, is worth noting.

Many smaller prizes and open shows are less selective. They may still provide useful exposure, but their significance as a signal is limited. It is always worth looking at who judged a prize and what became of earlier winners.

EARLY GROUP EXHIBITIONS

Before solo exhibitions, emerging artists typically appear in group shows, often curated by independent curators or held in artist-run spaces and project spaces. These exhibitions are an important part of the ecosystem. They show which artists other artists and curators are paying attention to.

Inclusion in a group show at a respected non-commercial space, or in an exhibition organised by a curator with a strong reputation, can be an early indicator that an artist is being taken seriously by their peers.

READING THE SIGNALS TOGETHER

No single signal is decisive. A promising degree show, followed by a competitive residency, a prize shortlisting and inclusion in well-regarded group exhibitions, forms a pattern. One isolated achievement does not.

The pattern matters because it shows that several independent groups of informed people, each applying their own judgement, have reached a similar conclusion. That is a more reliable basis for interest than any one result.

We also pay attention to what happens next. Do galleries begin to take an interest? Does the artist secure a first solo exhibition? Does critical writing follow? An artist whose early promise is followed by steady progress is in a very different position from one whose early attention fades.

THE CASE FOR BUYING EARLY, AND THE RISKS

Buying at this stage has real appeal. Prices are low, the choice is wide and collectors can follow a career from the beginning. Many of the most rewarding collections were built this way.

The risks are equally real. Most artists who show early promise do not go on to build substantial markets. Careers can stall for many reasons unrelated to the quality of the work. Works of art are not regulated investments, and work by artists at this stage can be particularly difficult to resell.

The sensible approach is to buy work you genuinely value, at prices you are comfortable with, and to treat any future increase in value as a possible bonus rather than an expectation.
