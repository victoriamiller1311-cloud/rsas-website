Lending a Work to an Exhibition: Loan Agreements, Insurance and Credit Lines

A request to lend a work to a museum or gallery is a compliment to the collector and often a significant moment for the artist. Lending can be straightforward, provided the arrangements protect the work at every stage.

No. 49  ·  Collection Care  ·  4 minute read

WHO ASKS AND WHY

Loan requests usually come from public museums and galleries preparing exhibitions, but also from commercial galleries organising selling shows or retrospectives, and from art fairs and festivals. Requests normally arrive months or years in advance, by letter from the director or curator, describing the exhibition, its dates and venues, and why the work is important to it.

QUESTIONS TO CONSIDER

  • The borrower. Is the institution reputable and experienced in handling loans?
  • The venues. Will the work travel to more than one venue, and in which countries?
  • Duration. How long will the work be away, including transit and installation?
  • Condition. Is the work fit to travel? A conservator's view is worthwhile for fragile works.
  • Selling exhibitions. If the exhibition is commercial, could your loan be presented in a way that suggests the work is for sale?

THE LOAN AGREEMENT

Every loan should be governed by a written agreement, usually prepared by the borrower. It should cover:

  • The work, with full details and images.
  • Dates, from collection to return.
  • Insurance, setting out who insures, the value, and the basis of cover.
  • Transport and packing, setting out who arranges them, to what standard, and whether a courier will accompany the work.
  • Environmental conditions, covering light levels, temperature and relative humidity in the exhibition space.
  • Security, covering display, invigilation and alarm systems.
  • Condition reports, prepared at each change of custody.
  • Handling, specifying who may install, move or adjust the work, and an undertaking that no treatment will be carried out without your consent.
  • Photography and reproduction, covering whether the work may be photographed for the catalogue and publicity, subject to the artist's copyright.
  • Credit line, setting out how your name, or anonymity, will appear on labels and in the catalogue.
  • Costs, which are normally the borrower's responsibility.

INSURANCE AND INDEMNITY

Loans are typically covered on a nail-to-nail basis, meaning from the moment the work leaves your wall until it is rehung. The value used for insurance should be agreed in writing and reflect current market value; a valuation may be needed. For loans to eligible UK public institutions, the Government Indemnity Scheme can take the place of commercial insurance. Check whether your own policy should remain in force during the loan, and tell your insurer about the arrangement.

COURIERS AND CONDITION REPORTS

For valuable or fragile works, the lender can ask for a courier, usually a conservator or registrar, to accompany the work and supervise packing, unpacking and installation. The borrower pays the courier's costs. Condition reports at each stage, with photographs, record the work's state and provide evidence if damage occurs.

CREDIT LINES AND PRIVACY

Many lenders prefer anonymity, credited as "Private collection". Others are happy to be named. Decide before the agreement is signed, because catalogues and labels are prepared early. Even when anonymous, your name will be known to the borrower, so consider confidentiality terms if privacy matters.

BENEFITS OF LENDING

Being exhibited and reproduced in a catalogue adds to a work's documented history. A work shown in a respected museum exhibition carries that history permanently, and the catalogue becomes part of its literature. Lending also supports the artist's reputation and allows the public to see work that would otherwise remain private.

WHEN TO DECLINE

It is reasonable to decline a loan if the work is too fragile to travel, if the terms are inadequate, if the period is too long, or if the exhibition is primarily a commercial selling show. A courteous refusal with reasons is normal practice.
