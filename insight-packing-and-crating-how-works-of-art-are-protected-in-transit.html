Packing and Crating: How Works of Art Are Protected in Transit

Works of art are most at risk when they move. Vibration, shock, sudden changes in temperature and humidity, and handling mistakes cause a large share of the damage conservators see. Professional packing reduces those risks considerably, and knowing what good packing looks like helps owners choose the right service.

No. 69  ·  Collection Care  ·  4 minute read

WHY TRANSIT IS RISKY

A painting on a wall is stable. In transit it may be lifted, tilted, stacked, vibrated in a vehicle, exposed to cold in a loading bay and to heat in a warehouse, all within a day. Canvases can be dented, paint can crack or flake, glass can break, frames can be chipped and sculpture can be scratched or broken. Many problems arise not on the road but at the moments of packing, loading and unpacking.

LEVELS OF PACKING

Fine art shippers use different levels of protection depending on the work, its value, its fragility and the journey:

  • *Soft wrapping.* Layers of protective materials, such as acid-free tissue, plastic sheeting, foam and corrugated board. Suitable for robust works on short, accompanied journeys in a specialist vehicle.
  • *Travel frames and cases.* A frame or case that holds the work without touching its surface, often used for paintings moving between locations.
  • *Crates.* Rigid wooden crates, lined with foam and designed to absorb shock and buffer temperature changes. Standard for valuable or fragile works, air freight and long journeys.
  • *Museum-standard crates.* Built to the specification of the individual work, with insulation, shock-absorbing systems and sometimes humidity control. Used for important works and exhibition loans.

PAINTINGS, PAPER AND SCULPTURE

Each type of work needs different care.

  • *Paintings* travel best upright, never face down with weight on the canvas. Unframed canvases need protection for the paint surface and the back of the canvas.
  • *Works on paper* under glass need the glass taped or replaced with acrylic glazing to prevent shattering. Large glazed works are particularly vulnerable.
  • *Sculpture* often needs custom supports that hold it at its strongest points. Fragile elements may need individual protection.

ARRIVAL AND UNPACKING

On arrival, crated works should usually be left unopened for a period, often at least 24 hours, so that the work and its packing adjust gradually to the temperature and humidity of the new space. Unpacking too soon can cause condensation or stress to sensitive materials.

Unpack carefully, on a clean surface, with enough people to handle the work safely. Keep the packing materials until the condition check is complete.

CONDITION CHECKS

A condition report before packing and another on arrival are the best protection if damage occurs. Photographs of the front, back and edges, with notes of existing damage, make any claim far simpler. Our guide to condition reports explains what they should include.

CHOOSING A SHIPPER

Ask any shipper:

  • What packing method they propose, and why.
  • Whether their vehicles are climate controlled and fitted with air-ride suspension.
  • Whether handlers are trained in fine art handling.
  • How the work will be insured, and whether cover runs from the moment it is collected until it is installed.
  • Who will carry out condition checks.
