Behavioural Biases When Buying Art: Anchoring, the Winner's Curse and More

Buying art is not only a financial decision. It is personal, emotional and often social, and that is part of its pleasure. It also means buyers and sellers are prone to predictable mistakes. Economists have studied these biases in art markets in some detail, and knowing about them is one of the simplest ways to make better decisions.

No. 70  ·  Buying  ·  4 minute read

ANCHORING

Anchoring is the tendency to rely too heavily on the first figure we see. In the art market, the most common anchors are auction estimates, previous prices and gallery price lists.

Research on art auctions has found evidence of anchoring. A study by Alan Beggs and Kathryn Graddy, published in the American Economic Review in 2009, found that the price a painting achieved at an earlier sale continued to influence the price at a later sale, even after market changes were taken into account. A later study by Graddy and co-authors, published in the Journal of Cultural Economics in 2023, found further evidence of anchoring and of loss aversion among sellers.

For buyers, the lesson is to form a view of value from several sources, rather than letting a single estimate or past price set the frame.

THE WINNER'S CURSE

In any auction, the winning bidder is the person who valued the lot most highly. If everyone's estimates contain some error, the winner is likely to be the person whose estimate was most optimistic. This is the winner's curse: winning can itself be a sign that you have overpaid.

The competitive atmosphere of an auction makes it worse. Bidding against a determined rival can turn a purchase into a contest, and the desire to win can override a budget.

The remedy is simple, if not easy: decide your maximum before the sale, including all costs, and keep to it.

LOSS AVERSION

People feel losses more strongly than equivalent gains. In the art market, this shows up most clearly among sellers, who may refuse to sell below the price they paid, even when the market has moved. Reserve prices set high to avoid a visible "loss" can leave works unsold, and an unsold work can be harder to sell afterwards. Our guide to what happens when a work does not sell explains why.

THE ENDOWMENT EFFECT

Owners tend to value what they own more highly than they would if they were buying it. A painting that has hung in a family home for decades can feel priceless. This is natural, but it can lead to unrealistic expectations when the time comes to sell.

SOCIAL PROOF AND FASHION

Buyers are influenced by what others are buying. Long waiting lists, sold-out exhibitions and attention on social media can create a sense of urgency. Sometimes this reflects genuine quality and demand. Sometimes it reflects a short-lived fashion. Our guide to spotting hype describes the warning signs.

A DISCIPLINED PROCESS

A few habits reduce the effect of these biases:

  • Research before you look at a price, not after.
  • Gather several views of value: auction records, gallery prices and independent advice.
  • Set a budget in advance and write it down.
  • Leave time between seeing a work and committing to it.
  • Ask why you want the work. If the answer is only that others want it, think again.
