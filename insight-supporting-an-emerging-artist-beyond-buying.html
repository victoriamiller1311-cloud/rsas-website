Supporting an Emerging Artist Beyond Buying

Buying a work is the most direct support a collector can give an artist. It is not the only one. Thoughtful collectors can help an early career in ways that cost little but matter a great deal, provided they respect the boundaries that keep the relationship healthy.

No. 45  ·  Emerging Artists  ·  4 minute read

LENDING TO EXHIBITIONS

When an artist is invited to show in a public gallery or an important group exhibition, the curator often wants to include works already in private collections. Agreeing to lend helps the artist present their best work and puts your collection in a public context. No. 49 in this series explains how loans work in practice.

INTRODUCTIONS

Collectors know other collectors, curators, writers and gallerists. An introduction, made with care and with the artist's agreement, can lead to a studio visit, an exhibition or a commission. The most useful introductions are specific: a curator working on a relevant theme, or a collector who already owns related work.

COMMISSIONS

Commissioning a work can give an artist the resources to make something ambitious that they could not otherwise afford to produce. No. 29 in this series covers commissioning in detail. The key is to give the artist room to work, with a clear agreement on budget, timing and what happens if the finished work is not what either side expected.

PATRONS' GROUPS AND MEMBERSHIPS

Many public galleries and artist-run spaces have patrons' schemes whose contributions fund exhibitions, residencies and acquisitions. Organisations that buy work by living artists for public collections offer memberships that combine support with access to studio visits and private views. These schemes allow collectors to support artists collectively, without the pressures of a direct relationship.

TALKING ABOUT THE WORK

Sharing an artist's work with friends, writing about it, or posting it online with proper credit all help build an audience. Always credit the artist and their gallery, ask before reproducing images widely, and remember that copyright stays with the artist, as No. 24 in this series explains.

BEING A GOOD OWNER

Look after the work. Frame and hang it properly, insure it, and keep its paperwork. Let the artist or their gallery know where it is, so it can be recorded in their archive and found if it is needed for a future exhibition or catalogue. A well-documented collection is a resource for the artist as well as the owner.

BOUNDARIES

Support works best when it does not come with conditions. Collectors should avoid:

  • Directing the work. Suggesting subjects, colours or styles, outside an agreed commission, puts pressure on an artist that can distort their practice.
  • Undermining the gallery. If an artist has a gallery, buying directly from the studio at a lower price can damage that relationship and the artist's market. No. 32 in this series covers primary market etiquette.
  • Quick resale. Selling a work soon after buying it, particularly at auction, can harm an artist's market at a vulnerable stage.
  • Expecting favours. Generosity that comes with an expectation of discounts, first choice or special access can make an artist feel obliged.
  • Blurring the relationship. Friendship between collector and artist is common and often valuable, but financial arrangements, such as loans of money, can complicate it.

PATIENCE

Artists' careers develop over decades, not seasons. The most valued supporters are often those who keep buying, lending and attending through the quiet years, not only when an artist is attracting attention. That patience is also the best protection for a collector against the temptation to treat an early-career artist as a short-term prospect.

How RSAS can help

RSAS helps clients build long-term relationships with emerging artists and their galleries, including commissions, loans and introductions, while keeping arrangements clear and properly documented.
