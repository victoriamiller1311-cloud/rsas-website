Tokenised Artworks After the NFT Boom: What You Actually Own

The market for non-fungible tokens, or NFTs, rose and fell dramatically in a few years. Serious artists still make and sell tokenised works, and some museums collect them. Before buying, however, it is essential to understand exactly what the token gives you, and what it does not.

No. 55  ·  Buying  ·  4 minute read

A SHORT HISTORY

Tokenised art became front-page news in March 2021, when Christie's sold Everydays: The First 5000 Days by the artist known as Beeple for about $69.3 million. A speculative boom followed, and prices for many tokens later fell sharply. The market today is smaller, more specialised and focused on artists with established practices in digital and generative art.

WHAT A TOKEN IS

A non-fungible token is a unique record on a blockchain, a shared digital ledger. It typically points to a digital file, such as an image, animation or piece of code, and records who holds the token. Transfers of the token are recorded on the ledger.

In most cases, however, the artwork file itself is not stored on the blockchain. It sits elsewhere, on a server or a decentralised storage network, and the token contains a link to it.

WHAT YOU OWN, AND WHAT YOU DO NOT

This is where buyers are often surprised. Holding a token usually gives you:

  • A record that you hold that token.
  • Whatever rights the artist or platform grants in the terms of sale, often a licence to display the work for personal, non-commercial use.

It does not usually give you the copyright in the work. As with physical art, copyright generally stays with the artist unless it is assigned in writing. Our guide to copyright explains the principle. Nor does a token guarantee that the file it points to will remain available, unaltered, forever.

Read the terms attached to the work before buying. They vary widely.

RISKS TO CHECK

  • *Where the file is stored.* If the link points to a server that is switched off, the token may point to nothing. Ask how and where the file is stored, and whether you receive a copy.
  • *Authenticity.* Anyone can create a token pointing to an image they did not make. Confirm that the token was created, or "minted", by the artist or an authorised platform, and check the artist's own published records.
  • *Platform risk.* Marketplaces can close or change their terms. Some have changed how artists' resale royalties are paid.
  • *Wallet security.* Tokens are held in digital wallets. Lost keys or a compromised wallet can mean permanent loss. Fraud using fake links and impersonated sellers is common.
  • *Tax.* The tax treatment of tokens and the cryptocurrencies often used to buy them can differ from that of physical art. Take advice.

COLLECTING DIGITAL ART MORE BROADLY

Tokens are only one way of selling digital art. Many artists sell video, software and generative works with certificates and files supplied directly, sometimes alongside a token and sometimes without one. Our earlier guide to video and digital works covers display and preservation. Whatever the format, the questions are similar: what exactly is being sold, what rights come with it, and how will the work be preserved.

HOW WE CAN HELP

We check the history of a tokenised work, the artist's records and the terms attached to the sale, and explain what rights and risks come with it. We do not buy, sell or hold cryptocurrencies on behalf of clients.
