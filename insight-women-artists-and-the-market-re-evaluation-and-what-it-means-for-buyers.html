Women Artists and the Market: Re-evaluation and What It Means for Buyers

In November 2025, Frida Kahlo's El sueño (La cama) sold at Sotheby's in New York for $54.7 million, overtaking the $44.4 million paid in 2014 for Georgia O'Keeffe's Jimson Weed/White Flower No. 1 as the highest auction price for a work by a woman. The record is one sign of a wider re-evaluation of women artists by museums, scholars and the market.

No. 64  ·  Research  ·  4 minute read

A LONG IMBALANCE

For most of art history, women artists faced barriers to training, patronage, exhibition and critical attention. Many were successful in their lifetimes and then overlooked by later histories. The result is a market in which women artists have historically been under-represented, both in the number of works sold and in prices achieved, particularly at the top.

HOW RE-EVALUATION HAPPENS

Market re-evaluation usually follows institutional attention. The pattern is familiar:

  • *Scholarship.* Researchers publish new studies, monographs and catalogues raisonnés.
  • *Exhibitions.* Museums mount retrospectives and group shows that bring artists to wider audiences.
  • *Acquisitions.* Museums buy works to address gaps in their collections.
  • *Gallery representation.* Leading galleries take on artists or estates and present their work at fairs.
  • *Market response.* Prices rise, sometimes sharply, as demand meets limited supply.

This process has been visible across many periods, from women painters of the seventeenth century to post-war abstract painters and surrealists, and many artists working today.

OPPORTUNITY AND CAUTION

Re-evaluation can be lasting. When it rests on serious scholarship, sustained museum attention and a body of work of real quality, it can permanently change an artist's standing.

It can also be uneven. Interest in a category can lift the prices of artists whose work does not support them, and a single record can draw attention away from the depth, or lack of it, in an artist's market. The same disciplines apply as with any artist: look at exhibition history, institutional collections, gallery representation and the evidence of repeat sales. Our guide to how we research an artist's market sets these out.

QUESTIONS FOR BUYERS

  • Is interest in the artist supported by scholarship and museum exhibitions, or mainly by market activity?
  • How much of the artist's work is available, and how much is held by institutions?
  • Are prices consistent across auction, gallery and private sales?
  • Is there a catalogue raisonné or recognised authority for authenticity?
  • For historical works, is the attribution secure, given that some works by women were once attributed to male contemporaries?

CONTEMPORARY PRACTICE

Many women artists working today are represented by leading galleries and collected by major museums. For collectors of contemporary art, the usual guidance on emerging artists applies, including attention to representation and resale restrictions. Our guide to gallery representation explains what representation signals.
