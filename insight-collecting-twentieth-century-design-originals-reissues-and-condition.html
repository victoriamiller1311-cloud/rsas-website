Collecting Twentieth-Century Design: Originals, Reissues and Condition

Furniture, lighting and objects by the great designers of the twentieth century are collected as seriously as paintings. Unlike a painting, however, a famous chair may still be in production today. Knowing the difference between an early original, a licensed reissue and an unauthorised copy is the starting point for any buyer.

No. 53  ·  Buying  ·  4 minute read

A DISTINCT MARKET

Design collecting covers a wide range: modernist furniture from the Bauhaus and its successors, French mid-century pieces, Scandinavian design, Italian lighting, studio furniture and much more. Specialist auction sales, dedicated dealers and design fairs serve the field, and prices for the rarest pieces can match those for significant paintings.

The market values documented originals made in the designer's lifetime, particularly early production and pieces made for specific buildings or commissions. It values later production far less, even when the design is identical.

ORIGINALS, REISSUES AND COPIES

Many celebrated designs have been in continuous or revived production for decades. Some are made under licence by companies authorised by the designer or their estate. These licensed reissues are legitimate, well-made products, but they are furniture to use, not collectors' originals, and they are priced accordingly.

Unauthorised copies are a different matter. Some are openly sold as replicas. Others are presented, deliberately or carelessly, as originals. Distinguishing them requires knowledge of materials, construction methods, manufacturers' marks and the way production changed over time.

HOW ORIGINALS ARE IDENTIFIED

Specialists look at several kinds of evidence:

  • *Construction and materials.* Joints, fixings, upholstery methods and materials changed over the years, and later production often differs in small but telling ways.
  • *Marks and labels.* Manufacturers' labels, stamps and inventory numbers help date a piece, although labels can be added or removed.
  • *Provenance.* A piece traceable to a particular building, commission or early owner carries strong evidence of its origin.
  • *Published records.* Catalogues of a designer's work, period photographs and manufacturers' archives can confirm what was made and when.

CONDITION AND RESTORATION

Design was made to be used, and most pieces show it. Wear is expected. What affects value is the extent of replacement and restoration. Original upholstery, finishes and components are generally preferred. Replaced leather, refinished wood, rewired lighting and new parts all reduce value, sometimes considerably, although some work, such as rewiring a lamp for safety, may be necessary.

Ask for a full account of any restoration, and photographs of the underside, joints and fittings. A reputable seller will describe replacements honestly.

PRACTICAL MATTERS

Vintage lighting should be checked by a qualified electrician before use. Some older materials, including certain plastics, foams and finishes, deteriorate with age and may need specialist care. Large pieces need careful packing and shipping. And pieces containing protected materials, such as some rare woods, may need permits to cross borders.

POINTS FOR BUYERS

  • Ask whether a piece is an original, a licensed reissue or a replica, and how the seller knows.
  • Request clear photographs of marks, construction and any repairs.
  • Check what has been replaced or refinished.
  • Compare the price with documented auction results for the same model and period.
  • Buy from sellers who give a written description and stand behind it.
