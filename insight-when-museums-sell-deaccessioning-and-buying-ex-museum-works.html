When Museums Sell: Deaccessioning and Buying Ex-Museum Works

Museums do occasionally sell works from their collections, a process known as deaccessioning. When they do, the sale can attract strong interest and, sometimes, strong criticism. For buyers, an ex-museum work can offer excellent provenance, but it may also bring questions about ethics, export and reputation.

No. 63  ·  Buying  ·  4 minute read

WHY MUSEUMS DISPOSE OF WORKS

Most disposals are routine. Museums review their collections and may transfer, return or sell items that are duplicates, outside their collecting policy, beyond repair or better suited to another institution. Proactive collections management of this kind is encouraged in the UK.

Selling works mainly to raise money is a different matter, and is treated far more strictly.

THE UK FRAMEWORK

Most UK museums work within the Museums Association's Code of Ethics and the UK Museum Accreditation Scheme. Together, these set an expectation that collections should not normally be treated as financially negotiable assets. Financially motivated sale is regarded as ethical only in rare and exceptional circumstances, assessed case by case, and the first expectation is that works should stay in the public domain, for example by being offered to another museum.

The consequences of breaching these principles can be serious. Arts Council England can remove a museum's Accreditation, which affects its access to public funding. Croydon Council and Northampton Borough Council both lost Accreditation for their museums after controversial sales, Croydon of Chinese ceramics in 2013 and Northampton of an ancient Egyptian statue in 2014.

Many national museums are also governed by their own statutes, which strictly limit when they may dispose of collection items.

OUTSIDE THE UK

Rules differ elsewhere. In the United States, for example, museum directors' associations set professional standards on how sale proceeds may be used, and relaxed some of those rules temporarily during the Covid-19 pandemic. Some European countries treat public collections as inalienable, meaning they cannot be sold at all.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR BUYERS

Buying a work sold by a museum has real advantages. Provenance is usually well documented, the work may have been exhibited and published, and title is generally clear. A museum provenance can add to a work's appeal.

There are also points to consider:

  • *Reputation.* A work sold in a controversial disposal may attract public attention, and some institutions and collectors prefer to avoid such works.
  • *Export.* A significant work sold by a UK museum may need an export licence to leave the country, and could be subject to an export deferral. Our guide to moving art across borders explains the process.
  • *Conditions of sale.* Museums sometimes attach conditions, such as continued public access or restrictions on resale. Read them carefully.
  • *Restitution.* Some disposals are returns to former owners or communities rather than sales. Works with contested histories need particular care.

EX-MUSEUM WORKS ON THE MARKET

Not every work described as "ex-museum" was sold by a museum. Some were on loan, some came from private collections exhibited publicly, and some descriptions are simply loose. Ask for documentation confirming when and how the work left the collection.
