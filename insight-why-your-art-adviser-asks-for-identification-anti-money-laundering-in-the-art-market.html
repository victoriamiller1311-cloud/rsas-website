Why Your Art Adviser Asks for Identification: Anti-Money Laundering in the Art Market

Buyers and sellers of art are increasingly asked to prove who they are and where their money comes from. These checks are a legal requirement in the UK, and they protect honest clients as much as they deter dishonest ones.

Buying  ·  4 minute read

Not long ago, a buyer could acquire a significant work of art with little more than a handshake and a bank transfer. That has changed. Clients of galleries, auction houses and advisers are now routinely asked for identification, proof of address and, in some cases, information about the source of their funds.

Some find this surprising, even intrusive. It helps to understand why the checks exist and what they involve.

WHY THE ART MARKET IS REGULATED

High-value goods that are portable, hard to value and often sold privately can be attractive to people seeking to disguise the origins of money. Art has all of those characteristics. Over time, governments and regulators concluded that the art market needed the same kind of safeguards long applied to banks, lawyers and estate agents.

In the United Kingdom, the Money Laundering Regulations were extended in 2020 to cover art market participants. Broadly, this applies to businesses that trade in works of art, or act as intermediaries in their sale or purchase, where the value of a transaction, or a series of linked transactions, amounts to the equivalent of 10,000 euros or more. It also covers certain operators of freeports that store works of art.

Businesses within scope must register with HM Revenue and Customs, which supervises them, and they must comply with a set of legal obligations. Failing to do so can lead to substantial penalties.

WHAT THE CHECKS INVOLVE

The core requirement is customer due diligence. Before completing a transaction within scope, a business must identify its client and verify that identity using reliable documents or data.

For an individual, that usually means photographic identification, such as a passport or driving licence, and proof of address. For a company, trust or other entity, the business must also identify the people who ultimately own or control it, known as beneficial owners, and understand the structure.

Where the business is acting for someone else, it may need to identify the person on whose behalf the purchase or sale is being made.

SOURCE OF FUNDS AND SOURCE OF WEALTH

In some cases, a business must go further and ask where the money for a transaction comes from, or how the client accumulated their wealth more generally. This enhanced due diligence is required in higher-risk situations, such as transactions involving certain high-risk countries, unusually complex arrangements, or clients who are politically exposed persons.

A politically exposed person is someone entrusted with a prominent public function, such as a senior politician, judge or senior official, along with certain family members and close associates. Being a politically exposed person is not wrongdoing. It simply means extra checks are required by law.

SANCTIONS SCREENING

Separately from money laundering rules, businesses must not deal with people or entities subject to financial sanctions. Clients are therefore usually screened against official sanctions lists. This is a quick process for most people, and it is a legal requirement.

ONGOING OBLIGATIONS

The obligations do not end once a client has been identified. Businesses must keep records of the checks they carried out, generally for five years. They must monitor relationships for anything unusual, and they must report suspicions of money laundering to the authorities.

The law also prohibits a business from telling a client that a report has been made. This is known as the tipping-off offence. It means a business may sometimes be unable to explain why it has paused or declined a transaction.

WHY THIS PROTECTS HONEST CLIENTS

It is natural to view these checks as an inconvenience. In practice, they offer real protection to honest buyers and sellers.

They make it harder for works connected with crime to move through the market, which reduces the risk that you unknowingly buy something that later becomes the subject of a legal dispute. They support the integrity of provenance records. And they help maintain confidence in the market as a whole, which benefits everyone who owns art.

A business that carries out these checks properly is showing that it takes its legal obligations seriously. That is generally a good sign about how it conducts the rest of its affairs. A business that offers to skip them, or seems unaware of them, is a reason for caution.
