Visiting an Artist's Studio: What to Look For and What to Ask

A studio visit is one of the best ways to understand an emerging artist. An exhibition shows finished work, selected and hung. A studio shows the process behind it: the materials, the false starts, the ideas in development and the discipline, or lack of it, that will shape a career.

Emerging Artists  ·  4 minute read

WHY VISITS MATTER

For an artist with a short exhibition record and little or no auction history, the studio provides evidence that is otherwise hard to find. It shows whether the work in a degree show or first exhibition was a single success or part of a developing body of work. It also reveals how the artist talks about their practice, which often says a great deal about their seriousness.

Studio visits are part of how we assess the emerging artists we follow. They complement, rather than replace, the other evidence set out in our guides to graduate shows and gallery representation.

ARRANGING A VISIT

Artists are busy, and their studio is their workplace. Arrange visits in advance, through the artist or their gallery if they are represented. Be clear about why you are coming. An artist will treat a visit differently if they know you are a collector considering a purchase, a curator, or simply interested.

If the artist has a gallery, it is courteous, and usually expected, to involve the gallery. Many representation agreements require studio sales to go through the gallery, and buying around it can damage the artist's relationship with the people who are building their career.

WHAT TO LOOK AT

Look beyond the finished pieces on the wall. Useful signs include:

  • A consistent body of work that develops over time, rather than unrelated experiments.
  • Sketchbooks, studies and works in progress, which show how ideas form.
  • The quality of materials, and whether the artist understands how they will age.
  • How the work is stored and documented, which says something about professionalism.
  • Evidence of recent work, not only pieces made for a degree show or a single exhibition.

Notice, too, how much work there is. An artist producing very large quantities, quickly, may be under pressure to supply a market. That is not necessarily a problem, but it is worth understanding.

WHAT TO ASK

Good questions are open and genuine. Artists can usually tell the difference between curiosity and a sales interview. Questions worth asking include:

  • What are you working on now, and where do you see it going?
  • Which artists, past or present, matter to you?
  • How do you choose your materials, and how do you expect the work to last?
  • Where has the work been shown, and what is coming up?
  • How do you document and number your work?

If you are considering a purchase, ask how sales are handled, whether prices are set by the gallery, and whether any resale conditions apply.

PRICE AND PURCHASE

Studio prices for a represented artist should normally match gallery prices. A discount offered for buying directly may put the artist in breach of their agreement. For artists without representation, prices may be negotiable, but be wary of buying cheaply in a way that undercuts the artist's future market.

Any purchase should be documented with an invoice that identifies the work, its date, medium and dimensions, and the price. Ask the artist to sign the work, if they normally do, and to provide a certificate where relevant.

RESPECTING THE SPACE

A few courtesies go a long way. Do not photograph work without asking. Do not handle works unless invited. Do not share images of unfinished or unexhibited work. Keep to the time agreed. And if you are not going to buy, say so kindly rather than leaving an artist waiting for an answer.

WHAT A VISIT CANNOT TELL YOU

A good studio visit is encouraging, but it does not predict a career. Many talented artists never build a substantial market, for reasons unrelated to quality. Works of art are not regulated investments and can be hard to resell. Buy because the work matters to you, at a price you are comfortable with.
