Paying for Art Safely: Invoice Fraud, Bank Details and Escrow

The most common way buyers lose money in the art market has nothing to do with fakes or price. It is paying the right amount to the wrong account.

No. 36  ·  Buying  ·  4 minute read

HOW INVOICE FRAUD WORKS

The pattern is well known in property and professional services, and the art trade is a target for the same reasons: large single payments, international parties and a lot of business conducted by email. A fraudster gains access to an email account belonging to a gallery, an auction house, a shipper or the buyer, or creates an address that looks almost identical. They watch the correspondence, wait for an invoice to be issued, and then send a message that appears to come from the seller. It says the bank details have changed, perhaps because of an audit or a new account, and asks for payment to the new details.

The message usually arrives at the right moment, uses the right names and refers to the right work. The buyer pays. By the time the real seller asks where the money is, it has been moved on.

THE WARNING SIGNS

  • A request to change bank details, especially one that arrives by email alone.
  • An account in a different name from the seller, or in a different country.
  • Pressure to pay quickly, or a suggestion that the work will be offered to someone else.
  • A slightly different email address, such as a changed letter, an added word or a different domain ending.
  • A request to keep the payment confidential or to avoid calling.
  • An invoice that differs in layout, logo or wording from earlier ones.

SIMPLE CONTROLS THAT WORK

  • Confirm bank details by telephone before the first payment, using a number you already hold or one taken from the company's official website, not from the email containing the invoice.
  • Treat any change of details as suspect until confirmed in the same way, however plausible the explanation.
  • Check the account name. The beneficiary should be the business named on the invoice. UK banks now check the payee name against the account and will warn you if it does not match. Do not override that warning without speaking to the seller.
  • Send a small test payment for large transactions and ask the seller to confirm receipt by telephone before sending the balance.
  • Pay a company, not a person. Payment for a work sold by a gallery should go to the gallery's business account, not to a director's personal account.
  • Keep records of every invoice, email and call.

ESCROW AND STAGED PAYMENT

For higher-value private sales, an escrow arrangement can protect both parties. The buyer pays into an account held by a neutral third party, such as a solicitor or a specialist escrow provider. The funds are released to the seller only when agreed conditions are met, typically delivery of the work and confirmation of its condition and paperwork. Escrow costs money and adds time, but it removes the need for either side to trust the other with the full amount.

Staged payment is a simpler alternative. A deposit secures the work, and the balance is paid on delivery or on completion of checks. The terms should be written down before any money moves.

CASH AND CRYPTOCURRENCY

Many galleries and auction houses limit or refuse cash, in part because of anti-money laundering rules. A seller who insists on cash, or on payment in cryptocurrency to a personal wallet, should prompt careful questions. No. 20 in this series explains why art businesses carry out identity checks, and No. 39 explains how to check the business you are dealing with.

IF IT GOES WRONG

Speed matters. Contact your bank immediately and ask it to contact the receiving bank to freeze the funds. Report the fraud to the police. Tell the seller, using a telephone number you trust. Preserve all emails and documents. The sooner a bank acts, the better the chance of recovering at least part of the payment, and UK rules on reimbursement for some types of authorised push payment fraud may help, depending on the circumstances.

A NOTE FOR SELLERS

Sellers have a part to play. State clearly on every invoice that bank details will never be changed by email, and give buyers a telephone number to confirm payment details. Consider securing email accounts with two-factor authentication. Every gallery that does this makes the whole market safer.
