A Short History of the Art Market, and Why It Still Matters

Many features of today's art market, from auction estimates to gallery representation, have roots going back centuries. A little history explains why the market works as it does, and why some of its habits are so hard to change.

No. 65  ·  Research  ·  4 minute read

ANTWERP AND THE DUTCH REPUBLIC

In the sixteenth century, Antwerp was one of Europe's great trading cities, and it developed one of the first dedicated markets for pictures. Painters produced works for sale on the open market rather than only on commission, and dealers emerged to sell them. In the following century the Dutch Republic saw an extraordinary output of paintings, bought by a broad section of society. Prices, specialisation by subject and the role of dealers all began to look recognisably modern.

LONDON AND THE AUCTION HOUSES

Auctions of pictures became common in London in the eighteenth century. Sotheby's traces its history to 1744, when it began as a book auctioneer, and Christie's to 1766. These sales created a public record of prices for the first time, and auction houses gradually took on the role of setting and publicising values that they still play today.

PARIS AND THE DEALER SYSTEM

In nineteenth-century Paris, the Hôtel Drouot, which opened in 1852, became a centre of auction activity. At the same time, dealers began to build artists' careers in a new way. Paul Durand-Ruel, who supported the Impressionists when the official Salon did not, is often cited as a model: buying works in depth, holding exhibitions, promoting artists abroad and building a market over many years. The modern gallery, representing living artists, owes much to this approach.

NEW YORK AND THE POST-WAR MARKET

After the Second World War, New York became the centre of the contemporary art world. Dealers there promoted American artists internationally, and auction houses expanded across the Atlantic. Evening sales of major works became public events, and record prices began to attract wide press coverage.

GLOBALISATION AND FAIRS

From the late twentieth century, the market became global. Art Basel, founded in 1970, grew into the leading fair, and fairs multiplied around the world. Auction houses opened in Hong Kong and elsewhere. New collectors from Asia, the Middle East and Latin America joined the market. Our guide to the art fair calendar shows how far this has gone.

THE ONLINE SALE ROOM

The most recent change has been online selling. Online bidding in live sales, online-only auctions and gallery viewing rooms grew steadily, and accelerated sharply during the Covid-19 pandemic, when physical sales and fairs were suspended. Many collectors now buy without seeing works in person. Our guide to buying art online covers the practical points.

WHY HISTORY MATTERS FOR BUYERS

History explains several features of the market that can puzzle new buyers:

  • *Opacity.* Private sales have always been private. Many prices are never published.
  • *The power of auction records.* Because auction prices have been public for centuries, they dominate how value is discussed, even though most sales happen elsewhere.
  • *Relationships.* From Antwerp to today, dealers have built markets through long relationships with artists and collectors. That remains true.
  • *Cycles.* The market has seen booms and corrections many times. Understanding that history encourages patience.
