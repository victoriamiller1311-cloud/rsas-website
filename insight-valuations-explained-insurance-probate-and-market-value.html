Valuations Explained: Insurance, Probate and Market Value

Owners are often puzzled to find that the same painting has been valued at quite different figures. Usually nothing is wrong. Each valuation answers a different question, and the right figure depends on what it is for.

Research  ·  4 minute read

WHY THE PURPOSE MATTERS

A valuation is an opinion of value at a particular date and for a particular purpose. Change the purpose and the answer changes, sometimes considerably. Before commissioning a valuation, be clear about why you need it. A good valuer will ask.

INSURANCE VALUE

An insurance valuation estimates what it would cost to replace the work with a similar one, bought from a reputable source, within a reasonable time. For most works this is the retail replacement cost, which includes the margin a dealer would charge and any premium or tax a buyer would pay.

As a result, insurance values are usually the highest of the three. That is appropriate. If a work is destroyed, the owner needs enough to buy a comparable one, not the lower sum they might receive by selling.

Insurance values should be reviewed regularly. Insurers commonly recommend revaluation every three to five years, and more often for artists whose markets are moving quickly. An out-of-date value can leave an owner under-insured, which may reduce any payout.

PROBATE VALUE

For inheritance tax in the UK, assets are valued at open market value as at the date of death. That is the price the work might reasonably be expected to fetch if sold on the open market at that time, between a willing buyer and a willing seller.

Probate values are usually lower than insurance values, because they reflect what the owner would receive rather than what a buyer would pay. They must, however, be realistic. HM Revenue and Customs can challenge values it considers too low, and a careless valuation can lead to penalties. Probate values also become the starting point for capital gains tax when beneficiaries later sell. Our guide to selling inherited art covers this in more detail.

FAIR MARKET VALUE AND SALE ESTIMATES

When an owner is thinking of selling, they usually want to know what the work might achieve. A dealer or auction house may give an estimate, but it is worth remembering that an auction estimate is a marketing figure agreed with the seller, not an independent valuation. Our guide to reading auction results explains why.

A market valuation for sale should consider recent comparable results, the current state of the artist's market, the condition of the work and the likely costs of selling. It may sensibly be expressed as a range rather than a single figure.

OTHER PURPOSES

Valuations are also needed for divorce settlements, gifts, loans to exhibitions, and use of a work as security for a loan. Each has its own basis. A valuation for one purpose should not be relied on for another without checking that the basis is appropriate.

WHO SHOULD VALUE

Choose a valuer with relevant expertise in the type of work, ideally with membership of a recognised professional body. In the UK, these include the Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors, which has an arts and antiques specialism, and established associations of fine art valuers and dealers.

Independence matters. A valuer who may later be asked to sell the work, or to buy it, has an interest in the figure. That does not make their opinion wrong, but it should be disclosed and weighed. For probate and other formal purposes, an independent valuer is usually the safer choice.

HOW VALUERS CHARGE

Valuers generally charge a fixed fee, an hourly or daily rate, or a fee per item. Fees based on a percentage of the value are discouraged in professional practice, because they give the valuer an interest in a higher figure. Ask for the fee basis in writing before work begins.

WHAT A GOOD VALUATION CONTAINS

A written valuation should set out:

  • The purpose and basis of value, and the date at which the value applies.
  • A clear description of each work, with photographs.
  • Comments on condition, where relevant.
  • The evidence relied on, such as comparable sales.
  • The valuer's name, qualifications and any interests to disclose.

Keep valuations with the work's other documents. They form part of the record a future buyer, insurer or executor will want to see.
