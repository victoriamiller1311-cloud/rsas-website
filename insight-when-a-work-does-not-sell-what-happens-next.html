When a Work Does Not Sell: What Happens Next

Not every work finds a buyer at the first attempt. An unsold work is not a disaster, but how you respond to it can affect its value for years. Here is what to understand and what to consider next.

Selling  ·  4 minute read

Most discussion of the art market concerns works that sold, often at prices that made headlines. Far less is said about the works that did not. Yet a meaningful proportion of lots offered at auction fail to sell, and many works offered privately take far longer to find a buyer than their owners expected.

If it happens to you, the most important thing is not to react hastily.

WHAT "BOUGHT IN" MEANS

At auction, a lot that fails to reach its reserve is said to be bought in. The reserve is the confidential minimum price agreed between the seller and the auction house, below which the work will not be sold. If bidding stops short of that figure, the auctioneer closes the lot without a sale.

The work then returns to the seller, or remains with the auction house while next steps are discussed. Depending on the agreement, the seller may still owe some charges, such as insurance, photography or a fee calculated on the reserve. It is worth knowing these terms before consigning.

WHY WORKS FAIL TO SELL

There are several common reasons, and identifying the right one shapes what you do next.

The estimate may have been too high. Optimistic estimates can attract consignments, but they can also put off bidders and leave the work stranded.

The market for the artist may have softened. Demand for an artist's work is not constant, and a market that was buoyant a year or two earlier may have cooled.

The work itself may be less desirable than others by the same artist. Size, subject, period and condition all affect demand. A minor or atypical work can struggle even when the artist's market is healthy.

The timing or venue may have been wrong. Too many similar works in the same season, a sale in the wrong location, or a sale category that did not suit the work can all reduce interest.

Or there may be a question about the work itself, such as a gap in provenance or a concern about condition, which made buyers hesitate.

THE "BURNED" WORK

In the trade, a work that has recently failed at auction is sometimes described as burned. The record of the failed sale is public, and potential buyers may wonder why nobody wanted it. As a result, offering the same work again too soon, especially at the same estimate, rarely succeeds.

This effect fades with time, and it is less severe for works with clear quality and strong documentation. But it is a real consideration and the main reason not to rush.

OPTIONS AFTER AN UNSUCCESSFUL SALE

You have several routes, and the right one depends on why the work did not sell and on your own circumstances.

Post-sale offers are common. Auction houses frequently receive approaches after the sale from buyers who did not bid on the day. If a reasonable offer comes, accepting it may be sensible.

A private sale is often the most discreet way forward. Selling privately through a dealer or adviser avoids a second public result and allows a price to be negotiated quietly with interested buyers.

Waiting is a legitimate choice. If the market for the artist is temporarily subdued, holding the work for a few years, perhaps until a significant exhibition raises interest, may produce a better outcome. You simply need to be prepared to keep, insure and store it in the meantime.

A different venue can help. A work that did not sell in one city or category may suit another, whether a different auction house, a specialist sale or an online sale with a lower estimate.

Reconsidering the price is sometimes necessary. If the market has spoken clearly, a lower estimate or asking price may be the realistic route to a sale. This is not easy to accept, but holding out for a price buyers will not pay rarely ends well.

QUESTIONS TO ASK YOURSELF

Before deciding, consider a few points honestly. Do you need to sell now, or can you wait? Was the estimate realistic in light of recent comparable results? Is there anything about the work's documentation or condition that should be addressed first? Would you be content to keep the work if the right offer does not come?

Your answers will usually point clearly towards one route.

REDUCING THE RISK NEXT TIME

Many failed sales can be prevented by preparation. Researching recent results carefully, setting a realistic reserve, choosing the right venue and season, and resolving any documentation or condition issues before consigning all improve the odds. Asking an auction house or dealer to explain exactly how they arrived at their estimate is always reasonable.
