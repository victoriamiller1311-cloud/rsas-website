Hanging and Lighting Art at Home

Where and how a work hangs affects how it looks every day and how well it survives. A few practical decisions at the start protect both.

No. 46  ·  Collection Care  ·  4 minute read

CHOOSING THE WALL

Before deciding what looks best, consider what is safest. Avoid walls that receive direct sunlight at any time of day, walls above radiators, fireplaces and wood burners, kitchens and bathrooms, and external walls in older houses that may be damp or cold. Hallways and stairways can work well but are prone to knocks. No. 06 in this series covers the wider effects of light, heat and humidity on works of art.

HANGING HEIGHT

A common guide is to place the centre of a work at around 145 to 155 centimetres from the floor, roughly eye level for a standing adult. Over a sofa or sideboard, leave a gap of 20 to 30 centimetres between furniture and frame so the work relates to what sits below it. Groups of works can be treated as a single block, with the centre of the group at the same height. These are starting points, not rules; dining rooms, where people sit, often suit a lower hang.

FIXINGS

The fixing must suit the weight of the work and the construction of the wall.

  • Solid walls take screws with suitable plugs. Picture hooks with angled nails are adequate only for light works.
  • Plasterboard walls need fixings designed for cavity walls, or better, a fixing into a stud or batten behind the board.
  • Heavy works should hang from two points rather than one, using D-rings on the frame rather than a single wire, which can stretch or snap.
  • Security fixings, which lock the frame to the wall, deter theft and prevent accidental dislodging, and may be required by insurers for valuable works.

For large or valuable works, use a professional art handler. The cost is small compared with the cost of a fall.

LIGHTING

Good lighting reveals a work; poor lighting can damage it.

  • Avoid daylight on the work. Use blinds, curtains or UV-filtering film on windows if a work must hang in a bright room.
  • Use LED lighting. Modern LEDs emit almost no ultraviolet light and little heat, making them far safer for art than older halogen or incandescent bulbs.
  • Choose a high colour rendering index. A CRI of 90 or above shows colours accurately. A warm to neutral colour temperature, around 2,700 to 3,500 kelvin, suits most domestic settings.
  • Mind the angle. A light source at about 30 degrees from vertical reduces glare on glazing and avoids casting the frame's shadow over the image.
  • Avoid picture lights fixed to the frame on valuable works, as they concentrate heat and light on the top edge.

HOW MUCH LIGHT

Light damage is cumulative. Museums limit light on sensitive works, typically to about 50 lux for works on paper, textiles and photographs, and around 150 to 200 lux for oil and acrylic paintings. You do not need museum conditions at home, but the principles apply: keep sensitive works in dimmer rooms, turn lights off when rooms are empty, and consider rotating works on paper so that none is on permanent display. An inexpensive lux meter will show how bright a wall actually is.

GLAZING AND REFLECTIONS

Works behind glass or acrylic can be difficult to see under strong light. Anti-reflective, UV-filtering glazing reduces reflection and protects the work at the same time. For works on paper, glazing should not touch the surface; a mount or spacer keeps it apart.

MOVING WORKS AROUND

Rehanging keeps a collection fresh, but every move carries risk. Handle works with clean, dry hands or cotton gloves, carry them vertically, hold the frame at the sides rather than the top, and never lift a work by its hanging wire. Rest works on padded blocks, never directly on a hard floor. No. 50 in this series covers larger moves.
