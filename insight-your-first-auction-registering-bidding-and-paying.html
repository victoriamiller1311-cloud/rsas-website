Your First Auction: Registering, Bidding and Paying

Buying at auction is open to anyone, but the process has its own rules and its own pace. Knowing them before the sale starts takes most of the pressure out of the room.

No. 31  ·  Buying  ·  5 minute read

BEFORE THE SALE

Every auction is governed by the house's conditions of sale, printed in the catalogue and published online. They set out the buyer's premium, how payment works, when title passes, what happens if you do not pay, and what the auctioneer will and will not stand behind. They differ from house to house and sometimes from sale to sale. Reading them is not a formality: by bidding, you accept them.

Most houses hold a public view in the days before a sale. Use it. A photograph will not show surface texture, the true colour of a work or a repair that catches the light. If you cannot attend, ask the specialist for a condition report and further photographs, including the back of the work and any labels.

REGISTERING TO BID

You must register before you can bid, whether in the room, by telephone or online. Expect to provide photographic identification and proof of address, and for higher-value lots, a bank reference or a deposit. Auction houses are art market participants for anti-money laundering purposes, so these checks are a legal requirement rather than a matter of preference. Register early, because checks can take a day or two and late registrations are sometimes refused for important lots.

FOUR WAYS TO BID

  • In the room. You receive a paddle number and raise it when you want to bid. The auctioneer will look for clear signals, so a definite gesture is better than a nod.
  • By telephone. A member of staff calls you a few lots before yours and bids on your instruction. Telephone lines are usually offered only above a minimum estimate, and must be booked in advance.
  • Online. Most houses now run live online bidding, either through their own platform or through a third-party site. Some third-party platforms add their own surcharge on top of the buyer's premium, so check the terms before you register.
  • Commission (absentee) bids. You leave a written maximum with the house, and the auctioneer bids for you up to that figure, only as far as the competition requires. If two identical commission bids are received, the earlier one usually takes priority.

IN THE SALEROOM

Lots move quickly, often at a rate of one a minute or faster. The auctioneer opens below the low estimate and climbs in increments set out in the conditions, usually about ten per cent of the current bid. The auctioneer may also bid on the seller's behalf up to the reserve; No. 02 in this series explains what that means for reading the room.

Decide your limit before the sale and write it down. That figure should be the total you are prepared to pay, so work backwards: deduct the buyer's premium, any VAT on the premium, any import VAT and any Artist's Resale Right royalty to arrive at the highest hammer price you can bid. It is easy to add one more increment in the moment and much harder to explain it afterwards.

When the hammer falls on your bid, a binding contract is formed. There is no cooling-off period for auction purchases made in the room, by telephone or by commission bid, so a bid should never be placed speculatively.

AFTER THE HAMMER

The house will issue an invoice, normally on the day of the sale. Payment terms are typically short, often seven days, and most houses accept bank transfer and debit card, with limits on card payments and cash. Pay only to the bank details on the house's own invoice and confirm them by telephone if anything looks different. No. 36 in this series covers payment safety in more detail.

Title usually passes only when the house has received cleared funds. Risk, on the other hand, may pass to you earlier, sometimes at the fall of the hammer or after a short period of free storage. Insure the work from the moment the conditions say risk is yours, not from the moment it arrives home.

Collection deadlines matter. After a set number of days, the house or its warehouse will charge storage and handling fees. If the work needs specialist packing or transport, book the shipper as soon as you have paid.

IF SOMETHING IS WRONG

Read the house's guarantee before you bid. Most major houses offer a limited guarantee of authorship for a set period, often five years, covering the heading of the catalogue entry in bold type. It rarely covers condition, attribution in the wording that follows, or works described with qualifying terms such as "attributed to". No. 40 in this series explains how to read those terms.

COMMON MISTAKES

  • Bidding on the strength of a photograph alone.
  • Treating the low estimate as the likely price.
  • Forgetting to add the premium and taxes to the hammer price.
  • Bidding against oneself by leaving a commission bid and then also bidding online.
  • Leaving the work uninsured between the sale and collection.
