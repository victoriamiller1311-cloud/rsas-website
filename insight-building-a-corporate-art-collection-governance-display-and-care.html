Building a Corporate Art Collection: Governance, Display and Care

Many companies own art. Fewer manage it well. A collection that begins with a few works chosen for a new office can grow, over the years, into a significant asset that nobody is quite responsible for. A little structure at the start avoids most of the problems that follow.

Corporate and Hospitality  ·  4 minute read

START WITH A PURPOSE

Companies collect for different reasons. Some want to create a working environment that people value. Some want to express their identity, or their connection to a place. Some support artists from a particular region or stage of career. Others are simply furnishing new premises.

Each purpose leads to different choices. Writing it down, even briefly, gives later decisions a reference point and helps the collection keep a coherent character as people and premises change.

AN ACQUISITIONS POLICY

A short written policy should cover:

  • The purpose and focus of the collection.
  • Who can approve purchases, and up to what value.
  • The annual budget, and what it covers.
  • How conflicts of interest are handled, for example where a director knows an artist.
  • Whether works will be bought, rented or both.

Many companies form a small committee, combining senior management, people with an interest in art and, often, an external adviser. A committee spreads responsibility and protects the collection from changes in personal taste.

BUYING AND RENTING

Buying gives the company full control and a lasting asset. Renting suits companies that expect to move, want to refresh their spaces, or prefer a predictable annual cost. Many combine the two, buying signature works for public areas and renting for offices and meeting rooms. Our guide to buying or renting art for a hotel sets out the trade-offs, which apply equally to offices.

Whatever the approach, buy for suitability rather than expected financial return. Works of art are not regulated investments, and their value can fall as well as rise.

DISPLAY

Art in a workplace has to live alongside people, furniture and daily activity. Plan placement with the same care as lighting or acoustics. Avoid direct sunlight, heat sources and humid areas. Use secure hanging systems in public spaces, and glazing that protects works on paper. Keep works clear of fire escapes, signage and safety equipment, and include them in fire risk assessments.

Labels help. A short note on the artist and the work encourages people to engage with what they see, and turns decoration into something closer to a collection.

CARE AND INSURANCE

Specialist fine art insurance usually provides broader cover than a general business policy, including accidental damage and transit. Values should be reviewed every few years. Agree who is responsible for condition checks, cleaning guidance for facilities staff, and calling in a conservator when needed.

THE INVENTORY

An up-to-date inventory is the foundation of good management. For each work, record the artist, title, date, medium, dimensions, location, purchase price, current value, condition, provenance and any copyright or loan agreements. Attach photographs and keep the original documents safe.

Without an inventory, works go missing during refurbishments, are left behind on moves, or are given away without proper authority. With one, the company knows what it owns and where it is.

ENGAGING PEOPLE

The best corporate collections are noticed and discussed. Some companies hold talks or tours, invite staff to choose works for their areas, or rotate works between offices. Others lend works to public exhibitions. Engagement builds support for the collection and helps justify its cost.

MOVES, MERGERS AND DISPOSALS

Collections are most at risk when a company changes. Before a relocation, plan how works will be moved, stored and rehung. Before a merger or sale, establish what the collection contains and who will be responsible for it.

A disposal policy sets out when and how works may be sold or given away, who must approve it, and how proceeds will be used. Selling works quietly, without documentation, can lead to disputes and reputational damage. Copyright and moral rights, explained in our guide to copyright, continue to apply after a work has been sold.

ACCOUNTING AND TAX

The accounting and tax treatment of art owned or rented by a business depends on the company's circumstances and should be agreed with its accountant before significant purchases are made.
