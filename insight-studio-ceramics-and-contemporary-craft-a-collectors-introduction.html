Studio Ceramics and Contemporary Craft: A Collector's Introduction

Studio ceramics and contemporary craft have moved from the edges of the art market towards its centre. Museums collect them, major artists work in clay, glass and textile, and prices for leading makers have risen. For many collectors the field still offers outstanding work at accessible prices, provided buyers understand how it differs from painting and sculpture.

No. 54  ·  Buying  ·  4 minute read

WHAT STUDIO CERAMICS MEANS

Studio ceramics are pieces made by individual artists or small workshops, rather than in industrial production. In Britain the tradition is often traced to the pottery established in St Ives in 1920 by Bernard Leach and Shoji Hamada, and it continued through makers such as Lucie Rie and Hans Coper, whose work is now widely collected. Today the field is broad, ranging from functional tableware to large sculptural works that sit comfortably alongside contemporary art.

Contemporary craft extends the same approach to glass, textiles, wood, metal and jewellery. The common thread is mastery of material and process.

WHERE TO BUY

Work is sold through specialist galleries, makers' studios, craft fairs and auction houses, several of which hold dedicated sales of studio ceramics. Collect, the international fair for contemporary craft and design presented by the Crafts Council, is a leading London event. Degree shows and craft residencies are a good place to find emerging makers. Our guide to graduate shows covers this route.

WHAT MAKES A PIECE VALUABLE

As with other art, reputation, exhibition history and museum recognition matter. Ceramics and craft add some considerations of their own:

  • *Marks.* Most studio potters use an impressed seal or signature. These help identify the maker and, sometimes, the period.
  • *Period.* Work from a maker's most admired years generally commands higher prices.
  • *Form and glaze.* Some forms and glazes are especially associated with particular makers and are more sought after.
  • *Scale.* Larger, more ambitious pieces are rarer and usually more expensive.
  • *Condition.* Chips, cracks and restoration have a significant effect on value.

CONDITION MATTERS MORE THAN YOU MIGHT THINK

Ceramics and glass can break, and damage is often hard to see. Hairline cracks, small chips to rims and foot rings, and invisible restoration are common. Ultraviolet light can reveal some repairs, and a careful examination under good light is essential. Ask the seller to describe any damage or restoration in writing. Our guide to condition reports explains what to request.

Some makers' work also shows features that are part of the making process, such as kiln marks, crawling glazes or slight irregularities. These are not faults, and a knowledgeable seller can explain the difference.

LIVING WITH CRAFT

Craft is often bought to be lived with, and many pieces can be handled and used with care. Display pieces securely, away from edges and busy areas. Use museum wax or discreet mounts for ceramics in homes with children or pets, or in areas prone to vibration. Glass and some glazes are sensitive to sudden temperature change, and textiles to light and pests.

POINTS FOR BUYERS

  • Check the maker's mark and compare it with published examples.
  • Ask about the piece's date and exhibition history.
  • Examine closely for chips, cracks and repairs, or ask for a detailed report.
  • Buy what you enjoy living with. Craft rewards close attention over time.
