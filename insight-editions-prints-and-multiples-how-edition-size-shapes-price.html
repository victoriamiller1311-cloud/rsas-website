Editions, Prints and Multiples: How Edition Size Shapes Price

Prints and editioned works offer a way to own work by artists whose unique pieces may be out of reach. Their value depends on details that are easy to miss, starting with how many exist.

Research  ·  4 minute read

A unique painting exists once. A print, photograph or sculpture cast in bronze may exist many times over. Editioned works are a large and lively part of the contemporary market, and they are often a sensible first step for a collector. They also come with their own vocabulary, and the details matter.

WHAT AN EDITION IS

An edition is a set of identical, or near-identical, works produced from the same matrix: a printing plate, a screen, a photographic negative or digital file, or a sculptural mould. The artist, or the publisher working with them, decides how many will be made.

In a limited edition that number is fixed and the works are numbered, usually as a fraction. A print marked 12/50 is the twelfth impression in an edition of fifty. An open edition has no fixed limit, which generally places it at the decorative end of the market rather than among collectable works.

WHY EDITION SIZE MATTERS

Scarcity plays a large part in price. All else being equal, a work in an edition of ten is likely to be worth considerably more than the same image in an edition of two hundred.

All else is rarely equal, though. The reputation of the artist matters more than the edition size alone, and so does the significance of the image within their work. An important print by a major artist in an edition of one hundred can be worth far more than a minor print by a lesser artist in an edition of five.

The number within the edition, whether 1/50 or 37/50, usually has little or no effect on value in contemporary printmaking, since modern printing methods produce consistent impressions. Some buyers still prefer low numbers, but that preference is sentimental rather than technical.

ARTIST'S PROOFS AND OTHER IMPRESSIONS

Beyond the numbered edition, there are often additional impressions. Artist's proofs, marked AP, are traditionally retained by the artist. Their number varies, but they are commonly a small proportion of the main edition. Printer's proofs, hors commerce impressions marked HC, and trial or working proofs may also exist.

These extra impressions matter for two reasons. They add to the total number of works in circulation, so the true size of an edition may be larger than the number on the label suggests. And some proofs, particularly working proofs that differ from the final edition, can be more desirable than numbered impressions.

When buying, it is worth asking for the full count: numbered edition, artist's proofs and any other impressions.

SIGNATURE, PUBLISHER AND DOCUMENTATION

A signed edition carries the artist's signature, normally in pencil for prints. Unsigned impressions, or prints with a signature printed into the image rather than added by hand, are usually worth much less.

The publisher matters too. Many editions are produced by specialist print studios or publishers whose names carry weight. A known publisher, an accurate catalogue entry and a certificate or documentation linking the work to the edition all support value and make resale easier.

For many established artists, prints are listed in a catalogue raisonné of the editioned work. If such a catalogue exists, a print should match its entry in dimensions, medium, edition size and paper.

MEDIA AND TECHNIQUES

Editioned work takes many forms. Etching, lithography, screenprinting and woodcut are traditional printmaking techniques, each with a distinct look. Photographs are usually editioned, and many contemporary photographers produce the same image at several sizes, each in its own small edition. Sculpture may be cast in limited numbers.

Digital printing, often described as giclée or pigment printing, is widely used and perfectly respectable when the artist has approved and signed the edition. It is also the technique most open to abuse, since high-quality reproductions of unique works can be produced in large numbers. A signed limited edition made with the artist's involvement is a different object from a reproduction of a painting, even if they look similar on a wall.

CONDITION AND CARE

Works on paper are vulnerable to light, humidity and poor framing. Fading, discolouration, foxing and staining from acidic mounts can all reduce value significantly. Photographs face similar risks. When buying, ask for a condition report, and when framing, use conservation-grade materials and glazing that filters ultraviolet light.

THE RESALE PICTURE

Editioned works do have a secondary market, and because many identical examples exist, their prices are easier to compare than those of unique works. That transparency helps buyers. It also means that when many impressions come to market at once, prices can soften.

As with any work of art, editions are not regulated investments, and their value can fall as well as rise. Buy them because the image and the artist matter to you, and check the details before you do.
