How We Research an Artist's Market Before Recommending a Work

A recommendation is only as good as the evidence behind it. This is how we assess an artist's market before we suggest a client buys, and why no single data point is ever enough.

Research  ·  5 minute read

Most buyers see the end of a research process: a work, a price and a reason to consider it. They rarely see what sits underneath. Yet the quality of that hidden work is what separates a considered acquisition from an expensive impulse.

Our name describes our method. Before we recommend a work, we build a picture of the artist's market from several directions at once. Each source has blind spots. Taken together, they tend to correct one another.

STARTING WITH THE ARTIST, NOT THE PRICE

Price is the last thing we look at, not the first. A number on its own tells you very little until you know what kind of career it belongs to.

So we begin with the artist's record. Where did they train, and when? Which galleries have shown them, and for how long? Have they been included in museum or institutional exhibitions, and were those solo shows or large group surveys? Has any public collection acquired their work?

None of these questions guarantees anything. They do show whether a career is being built by people who have staked their own reputations on it, or whether it rests on very little beyond enthusiasm.

REPRESENTATION AND THE PRIMARY MARKET

For a living artist, the primary gallery is usually the single most important influence on the market. It sets prices, chooses which collectors are offered new work and decides how quickly prices move.

We look at how long the current relationship has lasted and whether the artist has moved between galleries. A steady relationship with a respected gallery is a positive sign. Frequent changes can be innocent, but they deserve a question. We also note whether the artist is represented in more than one city, since a market supported in London and, say, New York or Berlin tends to be more resilient than one confined to a single dealer.

Primary prices are rarely published in full. Through gallery price lists, art fair reports and our own enquiries, we build a record of what new work has cost over time, by size and medium. Steady, measured increases usually indicate a gallery managing demand carefully. Sudden jumps can indicate the opposite.

THE SECONDARY MARKET RECORD

Once an artist's work begins to appear at auction or on the private resale market, a second set of evidence becomes available. Auction results are public, which makes them useful, but they need careful reading.

We record each result with its estimate, hammer price and premium, and we pay as much attention to works that failed to sell as to those that did. A run of unsold lots says something important. So does a pattern of results clustering at the low estimate. We also compare like with like: a large early canvas and a small recent work on paper are not the same proposition, even if they carry the same name.

Where an artist has no secondary market at all, we say so plainly. That is not a reason to avoid the work, but it changes the conversation. A buyer in that position is relying on the primary gallery's pricing and on their own conviction, with no independent test yet available.

CONSISTENCY, SUPPLY AND DEMAND

Two further measures are easy to overlook.

The first is consistency. We look at whether prices across the primary and secondary markets broadly agree with one another. A wide gap in either direction tells us something is out of balance, and we want to understand why before recommending a purchase.

The second is supply. How much work does the artist produce each year? How much of it is reaching the market, and through which channels? An artist whose output suddenly increases, or whose work starts appearing through many unconnected sellers at once, may face downward pressure on prices however strong the demand appears.

CRITICAL AND INSTITUTIONAL ATTENTION

Market data shows what people have paid. Critical attention can indicate what informed opinion thinks of the work itself. We read serious reviews, catalogue essays and curatorial writing, and we note which institutions have shown or acquired the artist.

We treat social media following and press coverage with more caution. Both can be helpful signs of growing interest. Both can also be manufactured, and neither substitutes for the judgement of curators and established critics.

WHAT WE DO WITH THE EVIDENCE

When the research is complete, we summarise it for the client in plain terms. We set out what the evidence supports, where it is thin and what remains uncertain. If a work looks fully priced, we say so. If the evidence does not support a recommendation, we do not make one.

This is the part of the process clients value most, and it is the part most likely to be missing elsewhere. A seller has an obvious interest in a sale. A research-led adviser should have an interest in the right sale, or in no sale at all.

THE LIMITS OF RESEARCH

Research reduces uncertainty. It does not remove it. Tastes change, careers stall, and markets can move for reasons no data set could have predicted. A thorough assessment improves the odds of a sound decision, but works of art are not regulated investments, and their value can fall as well as rise.

What research does offer is clarity. A buyer who understands the evidence behind a price is in a far stronger position than one who has been offered only a story.
