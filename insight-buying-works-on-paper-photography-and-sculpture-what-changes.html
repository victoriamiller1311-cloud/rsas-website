Buying Works on Paper, Photography and Sculpture: What Changes

Most buying guides assume the work in question is a painting on canvas. Works on paper, photographs and sculpture each raise their own questions about condition, authenticity and care, and those questions are worth asking before the purchase, not after.

No. 34  ·  Buying  ·  4 minute read

WORKS ON PAPER

Drawings, watercolours, gouaches, pastels and collages are often more intimate than paintings and more affordable for the same artist. They are also more vulnerable.

  • Light. Paper and many pigments used on it fade with exposure. Fading is irreversible. Ask how and where the work has been displayed, and look at the edges under the mount, where the original colour may be visible.
  • Foxing and staining. Brown spots caused by mould or impurities in the paper are common in older works. They can often be reduced by a paper conservator but rarely removed entirely.
  • Mounts and backing. Acidic card mounts and backing boards cause discolouration over time. Ask whether the work has been taken out of the frame and examined.
  • Medium. Pastel and charcoal sit loosely on the surface and can be smudged or dislodged by handling or by static from acrylic glazing.

Always ask to see a work on paper out of its frame, or at least to see the back. Labels, inscriptions and earlier mount markings can be important evidence of provenance.

PHOTOGRAPHY

A photograph's value depends heavily on facts that are not visible in the image itself.

  • Print date. A print made at the time the negative was taken, known as a vintage print, is usually worth considerably more than a later print from the same negative.
  • Edition and size. Many photographers issue the same image in several sizes, each with its own edition. An edition of five at large scale and an edition of twenty-five at small scale are different works for pricing purposes.
  • Process. The printing method matters. Silver gelatin, platinum and chromogenic prints behave differently over time, and some colour processes are prone to fading.
  • Signature and stamps. Check whether the print is signed by the photographer, stamped by an estate, or both, and whether the documentation matches.
  • Who printed it. Prints made by the photographer, under their supervision or by an estate after death are valued differently.

Ask for a certificate that records the edition number, the total edition, the size and the print date. No. 21 in this series covers certificates in detail.

SCULPTURE

Sculpture introduces questions of fabrication, edition and logistics.

  • Casts and editions. Bronze sculptures are typically cast in limited editions, plus a small number of artist's proofs. Casts made after the artist's death, sometimes called posthumous casts, are valued differently from lifetime casts. Ask the foundry, the cast date and the edition number.
  • Fabrication. Many contemporary sculptures are fabricated by specialist workshops to the artist's design. That is normal, but the certificate should confirm that the work was authorised by the artist.
  • Weight and installation. Large works may need structural assessment, specialist lifting equipment and a plinth. Outdoor works need suitable foundations and fixings.
  • Outdoor care. Patinas on bronze change with weather and can be damaged by pollution and cleaning products. Outdoor works need periodic maintenance by a conservator.
  • Transport. Sculpture often costs more to crate and move than its size suggests. Include those costs in the budget from the outset.

INSURANCE AND DISPLAY

Each category needs specific attention from your insurer. Works on paper are usually displayed behind glazing, which itself can break. Photographs may be face-mounted to acrylic, which scratches easily. Outdoor sculpture may need separate cover, and some policies exclude theft from gardens unless the work is properly anchored. Tell your insurer exactly what you have and where it is.
