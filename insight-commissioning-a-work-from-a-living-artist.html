Commissioning a Work from a Living Artist

Commissioning a work of art is one of the most rewarding ways to collect. It creates something that would not otherwise exist, made for a particular person, space or occasion. It also carries risks that buying a finished work does not, and most of them are avoided by agreeing the terms clearly at the start.

Emerging Artists  ·  4 minute read

WHY COMMISSION

Private collectors commission portraits, works for particular rooms or pieces to mark an event. Companies, hotels and public bodies commission works for lobbies, offices and public spaces, sometimes as part of a planning requirement. In each case the appeal is the same: a work that belongs to its setting.

The trade-off is uncertainty. You are paying for a work that does not yet exist. A good commission depends on choosing the right artist, briefing them well and agreeing a sensible process.

CHOOSING THE ARTIST

Look for an artist whose existing work already points in the direction you want. Asking an abstract painter for a portrait, or a painter of small intimate works for a large public piece, rarely ends well. Review previous commissions, if there are any, and speak to the people who commissioned them.

If the artist has a gallery, the gallery will usually manage the commission and take its share of the fee. That is normal and often helpful, as the gallery brings experience of contracts and logistics.

THE BRIEF

A clear brief sets out what you want and what you do not. It should cover the subject or theme, the size and medium, the location and its conditions, the budget and the timetable. For works intended for a building, include plans, photographs, lighting and any structural or safety constraints. Our guide to briefing an art consultant covers this in more detail for architects and designers.

Leave room for the artist. Most artists do their best work when the brief defines the problem rather than the solution.

STAGES AND APPROVALS

Most commissions follow a staged process:

  • An initial proposal or sketch, often for a modest design fee.
  • Approval of a developed design, model or maquette.
  • Production of the final work.
  • Delivery, installation and handover.

Agree in advance how many rounds of revision are included, who has authority to approve each stage, and what happens if you decline to proceed after seeing the design. A common approach is that the design fee is paid whatever the outcome, while larger payments follow approval.

PAYMENT

Payments are usually staged to match the process. A deposit on signing, often a third to a half of the total, is common, with further payments at approval and on completion. The deposit allows the artist to buy materials and commit time.

The contract should say whether the price includes VAT, if the artist or gallery is registered, and who pays for framing, transport, installation and insurance during production and transit. These costs can be substantial for large works.

COPYRIGHT AND MORAL RIGHTS

Paying for a commission does not give you the copyright. Under UK law, the artist keeps it unless it is assigned to you in writing. If you will need to reproduce the work, for marketing, publications or merchandise, agree a licence or assignment in the contract. Our guide to copyright explains the distinction.

Artists also have moral rights, including the right to object to derogatory treatment of the work. For site-specific pieces, agree in advance what happens if the building is altered or the work needs to be moved.

WHAT IF IT GOES WRONG

Even good commissions can stall. Artists fall ill, timetables slip, and sometimes the final work is not what the client expected. A good contract anticipates this. It sets a realistic timetable with some flexibility, says what happens to payments already made if either side withdraws, and records whether the client must accept a work that follows the approved design.

Most disputes arise from unclear expectations rather than bad faith. Time spent agreeing terms at the start is rarely wasted.

HANDOVER

On completion, ask for an invoice describing the work fully, a certificate or signed statement from the artist, care and maintenance guidance, and copies of any agreements on reproduction and moral rights. For corporate commissions, add the work to the inventory and insurance schedule.
