Catalogues Raisonnés: How They Work and How to Use Them

For many artists, the most important document a buyer can consult is not a certificate or an invoice but a book: the catalogue raisonné, a scholarly record of everything the artist is known to have made.

No. 33  ·  Buying  ·  4 minute read

WHAT A CATALOGUE RAISONNÉ IS

A catalogue raisonné is a comprehensive, annotated list of an artist's works, usually within one medium such as paintings, works on paper or prints. Each entry normally records the title, date, medium, dimensions, inscriptions, provenance, exhibition history and literature, with an illustration. The best catalogues also record works that are lost, destroyed or known only from photographs.

They are compiled by scholars, artists' estates, foundations or committees, often over many years. Some run to several volumes and are updated with supplements. A growing number are published online, which allows new works and corrections to be added without waiting for a new edition.

WHY IT MATTERS TO A BUYER

For artists who have one, inclusion in the catalogue raisonné is often the single strongest piece of evidence that a work is genuine. Major auction houses and dealers may decline to offer a work that is not included, and buyers may expect a discount for one that is excluded. A work's catalogue number becomes part of its identity and appears in sale catalogues, invoices and insurance schedules.

Absence from a catalogue is not proof that a work is false. It may have been unknown when the catalogue was compiled, or its owner may not have submitted it. But absence does mean that the burden of proof sits with the work, and resale will usually be harder until the question is resolved.

WHO DECIDES WHAT GOES IN

Each catalogue has its own process. Typically the owner submits photographs, technical details and provenance documents, and the committee or author examines the work in person. Some charge a fee; some do not. The decision may take months. Committees can decline to include a work without giving reasons, and their decisions are matters of scholarly opinion rather than findings of fact.

This has made authentication committees vulnerable to legal claims from owners whose works were rejected. As No. 21 in this series notes, several foundations stopped authenticating altogether around 2011 and 2012 for that reason. Where no catalogue exists, or no committee is active, buyers must rely on provenance, technical analysis and the opinions of recognised experts.

USING A CATALOGUE RAISONNÉ WELL

  • Find out whether one exists. Ask the artist's gallery or estate, or consult the specialist directories maintained by scholarly bodies and major art libraries.
  • Check the right edition. A work included in a later supplement will not appear in the original volumes.
  • Match every detail. Dimensions, medium, inscriptions and date should agree with the catalogue entry. Small discrepancies can be innocent, such as a measurement taken with or without the frame, but each should be explained.
  • Compare the illustration. The image in the catalogue should match the work in front of you, including details of composition and signature. A replica or a copy may match the description but not the image.
  • Follow the provenance. The catalogue's ownership history should connect, without gaps, to the person selling the work.
  • Note pending works. Some committees issue a letter saying that a work "will be included" in a forthcoming volume. Ask for that letter and keep it.

CATALOGUES FOR LIVING ARTISTS

Living artists rarely have a published catalogue raisonné, but most keep an archive of their works with photographs and inventory numbers. Many galleries maintain a similar record. Ask whether the work you are buying appears in the artist's archive and whether the gallery will confirm it in writing. For emerging artists, this is the nearest equivalent and it is worth securing at the point of purchase.

PRINTS AND EDITIONS

Print catalogues raisonnés record edition sizes, proofs, paper, printers and publishers. They are essential for identifying a print correctly, because unsigned or later impressions can look very similar to signed examples from the published edition. No. 14 in this series covers edition sizes and pricing.
