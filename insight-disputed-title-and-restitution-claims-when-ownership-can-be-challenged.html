Disputed Title and Restitution Claims: When Ownership Can Be Challenged

A buyer who pays the full price for a work usually assumes that it now belongs to them. In most cases that is right. But if a work was stolen, looted or sold by someone without the right to sell it, the buyer may not have acquired good title, however honest the purchase.

No. 42  ·  Research  ·  4 minute read

THE BASIC RULE

As a general principle in English law, a seller cannot pass on better title than they have. If a work was stolen, the thief had no title, and nor does anyone who later buys from the thief, even in good faith. There are exceptions and time limits, and the position differs in other countries, some of which give stronger protection to good-faith buyers. Because works of art cross borders, the law of more than one country may be relevant to a single dispute.

WHERE CLAIMS COME FROM

  • Theft. Works stolen from homes, galleries, museums or churches sometimes resurface on the market years later.
  • Wartime looting. Many works were seized, confiscated or sold under duress during the Nazi era, between 1933 and 1945. Claims by the heirs of the original owners continue.
  • Cultural property. Some countries restrict the export of objects of cultural importance, and objects removed in breach of those rules may be claimed back.
  • Unauthorised sales. A work consigned to a dealer may be sold without the owner's consent, or pledged as security for a loan, leaving competing claims.
  • Family disputes. A work sold by one family member may in fact have belonged to several.

HOW CLAIMS ARE RESOLVED

Many claims are settled by negotiation rather than in court. In the case of works lost during the Nazi era, the UK has a Spoliation Advisory Panel, which considers claims involving works in public collections and recommends fair and just solutions. Outcomes include return of the work, compensation, or shared arrangements in which the work stays in a collection with an acknowledgement of its history. Private owners and auction houses often reach similar settlements, frequently involving a sale with proceeds divided between the claimant and the current holder.

When a claim is made against a work offered at auction, the house will usually withdraw it until the matter is resolved. That can leave the seller with a work that cannot easily be sold until the dispute is settled.

CHECKS BEFORE YOU BUY

  • Provenance. Ask for the full ownership history, with documents. Pay particular attention to gaps between 1933 and 1945 for works made before the end of the war, and to recent changes of ownership. No. 05 in this series sets out the questions to ask.
  • Stolen art databases. Specialist registers record stolen and missing works and can be searched for a fee. A search certificate is not a guarantee, because not every theft is reported, but it is a sensible step for valuable works.
  • Export history. For works that have moved between countries, ask whether export licences were obtained and keep copies.
  • The seller's authority. Confirm that the seller owns the work or has written authority to sell it. If the seller is acting for someone else, ask who.
  • Contractual protection. Your purchase terms should include a warranty of title from the seller, with a remedy if a valid claim emerges.

IF A CLAIM IS MADE AGAINST YOUR WORK

Do not ignore it, and do not move or sell the work in a hurry. Take legal advice from a solicitor with experience of art disputes. Gather your purchase documents, correspondence and provenance records. Notify your insurer, as some policies cover title disputes and others do not. Specialist title insurance is available for works of art, and may be worth considering for valuable purchases with incomplete histories.

LIVING ARTISTS AND RECENT WORKS

Title disputes are less common for works bought directly from a living artist or their representing gallery, because the chain of ownership is short and documented. They are not unknown. Works lent to exhibitions or held on consignment are sometimes sold without authority, which is why a clear invoice from the person entitled to sell is essential even for recent works.
