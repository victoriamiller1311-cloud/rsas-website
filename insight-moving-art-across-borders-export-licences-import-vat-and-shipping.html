Moving Art Across Borders: Export Licences, Import VAT and Shipping

Art moves internationally more than ever. Buyers acquire works from galleries abroad, sell through auctions in other countries, and relocate collections when they move home. Each crossing raises three separate questions: whether the work may leave, what tax is due when it arrives, and how to move it safely.

Buying  ·  5 minute read

This guide reflects the UK position, with notes on the European Union, at the time of writing. Rules change, and specialist advice is essential for valuable or older works.

CAN THE WORK LEAVE? UK EXPORT LICENSING

The UK controls the export of certain cultural objects under the Export of Objects of Cultural Interest (Control) Order 2003. The system is administered by the Export Licensing Unit at Arts Council England.

A licence is generally needed only where a work is more than 50 years old and its value exceeds a threshold set for its category. For most categories of object over 50 years old, the threshold is £65,000. Some categories, such as archaeological material, have lower thresholds or none at all, and certain categories of painting have higher ones. Below the relevant threshold, an Open General Export Licence usually applies and no individual application is needed.

For most contemporary art the question does not arise, because the work is less than 50 years old. Buyers of post-war and modern works should check, however, as many now pass the 50-year mark.

Where an individual licence is required, a work judged to be of outstanding national importance, under the long-standing Waverley criteria, can have its licence deferred. That gives UK institutions time to raise funds to buy it at a fair price. The process can take many months and should be considered before any sale abroad.

LEAVING OTHER COUNTRIES

Other countries have their own export rules, and some are much stricter than the UK's. Spain, Italy and France, for example, protect works forming part of their national heritage, and exporting such a work without permission can lead to confiscation and criminal penalties. If you buy a work abroad, ask the seller to confirm in writing that any export licence needed has been obtained.

The European Union also now controls the import of certain older cultural goods. Under rules that took full effect in June 2025, some categories of object over 200 years old entering the EU need an import licence or importer statement. This rarely affects contemporary art but matters for older works.

WHAT TAX IS DUE ON ARRIVAL? IMPORT VAT

Original works of art are generally free of customs duty in both the UK and the EU. Import VAT is the main cost.

In the UK, works of art, antiques and collectors' items qualify for a reduced import VAT rate, giving an effective rate of 5 per cent on the value. In Spain, imports of works of art, antiques and collectors' items carry VAT at the reduced rate of 10 per cent. Other EU countries apply their own rates.

Since the UK left the EU, works moving between the UK and EU countries are imports and exports, with customs declarations on each side. A work sent from London to a buyer in Spain will usually be exported free of UK VAT and then attract Spanish import VAT on arrival.

Gibraltar sits outside the EU customs union and VAT area, so works crossing between Gibraltar and Spain also pass through customs. Buyers based in the region should take advice on the specific route.

TEMPORARY MOVEMENTS

Works travelling for exhibitions, fairs or repair can often move under temporary admission or temporary export arrangements, which suspend import VAT provided the work returns within the permitted period. An ATA Carnet, an international customs document, can simplify temporary movements through several countries. The conditions are strict, and a work that stays beyond the deadline, or is sold while abroad, will usually become liable to tax.

PROTECTED MATERIALS

Works containing materials from protected species need separate permits under the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species, known as CITES. Ivory, tortoiseshell, certain rare woods and some feathers and skins are common examples, and they can appear in frames, bases and mixed-media works as well as sculpture. Without the right permits a work can be seized at the border, whatever its artistic value.

SHIPPING SAFELY

The physical move is where most damage happens. Fine art shippers provide specialist crating, climate-controlled transport and trained handlers, and they understand customs procedures. General carriers usually do not.

Points to agree before a work moves:

  • Who arranges the shipment, and who pays for it.
  • When ownership and risk pass from seller to buyer.
  • Whether the work is insured from the moment it leaves the wall until it is hung again, often called nail-to-nail cover.
  • A condition report before packing and on arrival.
  • Who is responsible for customs declarations, licences and tax.

DOCUMENTS TO KEEP

Keep copies of export licences, customs entries, import VAT receipts and shipping records with the work's other papers. A future buyer or auction house may ask how and when the work entered the country, and gaps can delay or prevent a sale.

HOW WE CAN HELP

We can set out the likely licences, taxes and shipping costs before you buy or sell across borders, and introduce specialist shippers and customs agents. We do not give tax or legal advice, and for complex or high-value movements we will recommend that you take it.
