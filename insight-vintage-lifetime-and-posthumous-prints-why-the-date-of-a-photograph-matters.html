Vintage, Lifetime and Posthumous Prints: Why the Date of a Photograph Matters

Two prints of the same photograph, made from the same negative, can differ in price by a factor of a hundred or more. The difference usually lies in when, and by whom, each print was made. For anyone buying photography, understanding print dates is the most important single skill.

No. 52  ·  Buying  ·  4 minute read

ONE NEGATIVE, MANY PRINTS

A painting exists once. A photograph can be printed many times, over many years, by the photographer, an assistant, a printer working after the photographer's death, or a commercial laboratory. Each print is a separate object, and the market values them very differently.

THE MAIN CATEGORIES

Terms vary between auction houses and dealers, and there is no single legal definition. Broadly, however, the market uses these categories:

  • *Vintage print.* Made at or close to the time the negative was taken, usually by or under the supervision of the photographer. Vintage prints are generally the most valuable, because they show the image as the photographer first realised it.
  • *Lifetime or later print.* Made during the photographer's lifetime, but some years after the negative. Many are signed and of excellent quality, but they usually sell for less than vintage prints.
  • *Posthumous or estate print.* Made after the photographer's death, often by an estate or authorised printer. These may be stamped rather than signed, and are usually priced well below lifetime prints.
  • *Modern edition.* A numbered edition produced in recent years, perhaps by a living photographer or an estate, often in a larger size than the photographer ever printed.

When a catalogue entry gives two dates, such as "1932, printed 1970s", the second date is the one that matters most for price.

HOW PRINTS ARE DATED

Specialists date prints by examining the paper, the chemistry and the marks on the back. Photographic papers changed over the twentieth century, and many papers made after the early 1950s contain optical brighteners that glow under ultraviolet light. Stamps, inscriptions, labels and the photographer's own handwriting all help. So does provenance: a print that came directly from the photographer's studio or a contemporary publication is easier to place.

Dating is not always certain. A careful catalogue will say "printed later" or "printed circa" where the evidence is incomplete. Treat confident dates without explanation with caution.

WHAT DRIVES PRICE

Beyond date, photography prices depend on the fame of the image, the quality of the print, its condition, its size and its rarity. A vintage print of a celebrated image in fine condition can reach very high prices. In 2022, a vintage print of Man Ray's Le Violon d'Ingres sold at Christie's in New York for about $12.4 million, a record for a photograph at auction.

Most photography trades at far more modest levels, and good vintage prints by respected but less famous photographers can still be found at accessible prices.

CONDITION AND CARE

Photographs are sensitive to light, humidity and handling. Silver gelatin prints can develop silvering at the edges, colour prints can fade or shift, and creases and surface abrasion are common. Ask for a condition report, and display photographs away from direct light. Our guide to condition reports covers what to ask for.

POINTS FOR BUYERS

  • Ask when the print was made, not only when the photograph was taken.
  • Look for evidence: paper type, stamps, inscriptions and provenance.
  • Check the size against the photographer's known printing practice.
  • For modern editions, confirm the edition size and whether other sizes exist. Our guide to editions explains why this matters.
  • Be cautious of unsigned prints described as vintage without supporting evidence.
