Collecting Old Master Paintings: Supply, Scholarship and Condition

Old Master paintings, broadly European works made before about 1800, sit in a market quite unlike contemporary art. No new works will ever be made, scholarship can transform a painting's value overnight, and condition is often the deciding factor in price. Buyers who understand these three forces make better decisions.

No. 51  ·  Research  ·  4 minute read

A SUPPLY THAT ONLY SHRINKS

Every year, some of the finest Old Master paintings leave the market for good. Museums buy them, collectors bequeath them to public institutions, and governments block their export. Few ever return to private hands. The result is a market in which truly important works are rare, and in which a great deal of what is offered is of secondary quality, uncertain attribution or compromised condition.

This scarcity explains much of the market's character. Top works attract intense competition. The middle of the market is thinner and more cautious. And buyers at every level need to judge carefully whether a work's description matches what is actually on the wall.

WHERE THE MARKET MEETS

The Old Master market is concentrated. The major auction houses hold their principal sales in London, usually in July and December, and in New York early in the year. Specialist dealers, many of them family firms with generations of expertise, handle a large share of private sales. TEFAF Maastricht, held each March, is the leading fair, and every work shown is examined by vetting committees before the fair opens.

For a new buyer, the dealers and the fair are often the best place to learn. Good dealers will explain attribution, condition and price history in detail, and will stand behind what they sell.

SCHOLARSHIP MOVES PRICES

With Old Masters, who painted the work matters more than almost anything else. A painting accepted as by the master himself can be worth many times a similar work described as by his studio, his circle or a later follower. Those descriptions are not casual. They reflect the opinion of specialists and, often, a small number of recognised authorities on a particular artist.

Opinions change. New research, technical analysis or the publication of a catalogue raisonné can upgrade a work or downgrade it. The most famous recent example is the Salvator Mundi, attributed to Leonardo da Vinci, which sold at Christie's in 2017 for about $450 million and whose attribution has remained the subject of public debate among scholars. The lesson for buyers at any level is the same: understand who supports an attribution, on what evidence, and whether anyone dissents.

CONDITION IS CENTRAL

Paintings several centuries old have almost always been restored. Canvases have been relined, varnishes removed and replaced, and losses filled and retouched. None of this is unusual. What matters is how much of the original paint survives, and how well earlier restoration was done.

Two paintings by the same artist, of similar size and subject, can differ hugely in value because one is well preserved and the other heavily overpainted. Ultraviolet examination, which reveals later retouching, and a report by an independent conservator are standard steps before any significant purchase. Our guide to condition reports explains what such a report should cover.

SLEEPERS AND REDISCOVERIES

The Old Master market is famous for "sleepers": works catalogued modestly at a smaller sale that turn out to be by a major artist. These discoveries make headlines, but they are rare, and they usually go to specialists who have spent decades studying a single artist. For most buyers, hoping to find a sleeper is a poor strategy. Paying a fair price for a well-documented work is a better one.

POINTS FOR BUYERS

  • Ask exactly how the work is attributed, and by whom.
  • Request a condition report, with ultraviolet photographs, from an independent conservator.
  • Check provenance carefully, including the period from 1933 to 1945. Our guide to provenance explains why.
  • Confirm whether an export licence will be needed if the work is to leave the country it is in.
  • Buy for quality and condition first. A modest work in excellent condition is often a better purchase than an important name in poor condition.
