Conservation and Restoration: Choosing a Conservator and What Treatment Means

Every work of art changes over time. Varnish yellows, paper discolours, paint lifts and frames loosen. Sooner or later most collectors face a decision about treatment, and the choice of who carries it out matters as much as the treatment itself.

No. 47  ·  Collection Care  ·  4 minute read

CONSERVATION AND RESTORATION

The two words are often used interchangeably, but they describe different emphases. Conservation aims to stabilise a work and slow its deterioration, with the least possible intervention. Restoration aims to return a work towards an earlier appearance, for example by filling and retouching losses. Professional conservators do both, guided by principles that favour minimal, reversible and documented treatment. A treatment that cannot be undone, or that changes the artist's original material, can permanently affect the work and its value.

WHEN TREATMENT IS NEEDED

Some problems need prompt attention:

  • Flaking or lifting paint, which can lead to permanent loss.
  • Tears, punctures and dents in canvas.
  • Active mould on paper or canvas.
  • Insect damage to frames, paper or textiles.
  • Water or fire damage. No. 48 in this series covers what to do after an accident.

Others are cosmetic and can wait: a discoloured varnish, surface dirt, or a tired frame. A conservator can advise whether treatment is necessary, desirable or best avoided.

CHOOSING A CONSERVATOR

Conservation is a specialised profession, usually divided by material: easel paintings, works on paper, photographs, sculpture, textiles, frames and modern materials each have their own specialists. Look for:

  • Professional accreditation. In the UK, the Institute of Conservation maintains a register of conservators, and accredited members use the post-nominals ACR.
  • Relevant experience. Ask about previous work on similar materials, periods or artists.
  • References from galleries, museums or collectors.
  • Insurance. The conservator should hold insurance covering works in their care.
  • Clear communication. A good conservator explains what they will do, why, and what the risks are.

Be cautious of general restorers who offer quick, inexpensive treatments for all types of work, and of anyone who proposes aggressive cleaning or extensive repainting without first examining the work closely.

THE TREATMENT PROPOSAL

Before any work begins, ask for a written proposal. It should include:

  • A condition assessment, describing the current state of the work.
  • The proposed treatment, step by step, with the materials to be used.
  • Alternatives, including the option of doing nothing.
  • Risks, and what could go wrong.
  • Cost and timescale.
  • Documentation, meaning photographs before, during and after treatment, and a written report.

Keep the report with the work's other papers. It forms part of the work's history and will be useful to future owners, insurers and buyers.

TREATMENT AND VALUE

Sympathetic, well-documented conservation usually supports a work's value, because it preserves the original and gives buyers confidence. Over-restoration does the opposite. Extensive retouching, heavy relining or the removal of original surfaces can reduce value substantially, and buyers increasingly ask to see conservation reports. Before treating a work you may sell, discuss the effect on its market with an adviser as well as the conservator.

LIVING ARTISTS

If the artist is alive, consult them or their gallery before treatment. Many artists have firm views about how their work should be cared for, and some use materials that need specialist knowledge. Unauthorised changes to a work can raise questions under the artist's moral rights, as No. 24 in this series explains.

PREVENTION

The best conservation is the treatment that is never needed. Stable conditions, careful handling, appropriate framing and sensible lighting prevent most damage. No. 46 in this series covers hanging and lighting.
