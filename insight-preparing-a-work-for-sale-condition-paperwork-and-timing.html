Preparing a Work for Sale: Condition, Paperwork and Timing

The price a work achieves depends partly on decisions made before it ever reaches a buyer. Careful preparation can protect value. Poor preparation can cost far more than the effort it would have taken.

Selling  ·  4 minute read

Once you have decided to sell, the temptation is to move quickly. A little preparation first is almost always worthwhile. Buyers, auction specialists and dealers all make judgements based on what they can see and verify, and the stronger your position on both counts, the better your result is likely to be.

GATHER THE PAPERWORK FIRST

Documentation is the foundation of any sale. Before approaching a buyer or auction house, collect everything you have relating to the work.

That includes the original invoice or receipt, any certificate of authenticity, correspondence with the gallery or artist, exhibition and publication history, previous condition reports, insurance valuations and records of any conservation work. Photographs of the work in a previous home or exhibition can help too.

Each document strengthens the provenance, which is the work's documented history of ownership. Clear provenance reassures buyers, reduces the risk of disputes and supports price. Gaps in the record invite questions and may reduce what a buyer is willing to pay.

If documents are missing, it is often possible to fill the gaps. The original gallery may hold sales records, and for artists with a catalogue raisonné, the work may already be listed. It is better to do this before marketing the work than to discover the problem when a buyer asks.

ASSESS CONDITION HONESTLY

Condition affects value directly, and buyers will check it. Look at the work in good light, front and back. Note any damage, discolouration, cracking, flaking paint, tears, stains, or signs of previous repair. For works on paper, look for fading, foxing or discolouration from acidic mounts.

For a work of significant value, an independent condition report from a qualified conservator is a sensible investment. It gives you an accurate picture before you set expectations, and it can be shared with prospective buyers.

Whether to restore before selling is a judgement call. Minor cleaning or remedial work can sometimes improve value. Inappropriate restoration can reduce it considerably, and some buyers prefer to commission their own conservator. Take advice before arranging any treatment, and keep a record of anything that is done.

THINK ABOUT PRESENTATION

Presentation does not change the work itself, but it does change how it is received. A damaged or unsuitable frame can make a good work look neglected. Good photographs, taken in even light with accurate colour, matter greatly for online and private sales.

For auction, the house will photograph the work and write the catalogue entry. You can still help by providing accurate information and any useful context from your documentation.

RESEARCH THE MARKET BEFORE SETTING EXPECTATIONS

Before you agree a sale route or a price, understand where the artist's market currently stands. Look at recent auction results for comparable works, taking size, medium, date and condition into account. Note unsold lots as well as successful sales. If the artist is represented by a gallery, find out what new work currently costs.

This research helps you judge whether an estimate or offer is reasonable. It also helps you decide whether now is a good moment to sell at all.

CHOOSE YOUR TIMING CAREFULLY

Timing matters more than many sellers realise.

Auction houses hold their main contemporary sales at particular points in the year, and works are consigned months in advance. Missing a deadline can mean waiting for the next suitable sale.

Market conditions matter too. If an artist has a major exhibition coming up, interest may rise. If several similar works have recently come to auction, buyers' appetite may already be satisfied, and adding another can push prices down. If a recent result was disappointing, it may be wise to wait.

Personal circumstances often decide timing, and that is entirely legitimate. But where you have flexibility, using it can make a real difference.

UNDERSTAND THE COSTS

Selling a work of art involves costs. At auction these typically include a seller's commission and may include charges for photography, insurance and transport. Private sales and consignment arrangements carry their own fees. The Artist's Resale Right may apply to resales of work by living or recently deceased artists. Tax may also be due on any gain.

Ask for a full breakdown before you commit, and calculate what you expect to receive after all costs rather than focusing only on the headline price. Take professional tax advice where needed.

AGREE THE TERMS IN WRITING

Whichever route you choose, make sure the terms are clear and written down: the commission, the reserve or minimum price, how long the arrangement lasts, who pays for transport and insurance, and what happens if the work does not sell. A clear agreement protects both parties.
