When a Work Is Damaged: Insurance Claims, Loss Adjusters and Repair

Accidents happen: a work falls from the wall, a pipe leaks, a removal goes wrong. What you do in the first hours can make a real difference to the outcome, both for the work and for the claim.

No. 48  ·  Collection Care  ·  4 minute read

FIRST STEPS

  • Make the area safe. Stop the source of damage if you can, such as by turning off water or clearing broken glass.
  • Do not attempt repairs. Do not clean, dry with heat, flatten, glue or retouch. Well-meant first aid often causes more harm than the original accident.
  • Photograph everything before moving anything: the work, the location, the cause, and any fragments.
  • Keep every fragment. Flakes of paint, pieces of frame and shards of glass may be needed for repair. Place them in labelled envelopes or containers.
  • Move the work only if it is at further risk, and then carry it flat or upright as appropriate, with any glass removed or taped.

WET WORKS

Water damage needs fast, careful handling. Lift the work out of standing water. For works on paper and photographs, contact a conservator immediately, as mould can develop within a few days. Do not use hairdryers, radiators or direct sunlight to dry anything. A conservator may advise freezing certain works on paper to stop mould while treatment is arranged.

TELL YOUR INSURER

Notify your insurer as soon as possible, and in any case within the period stated in your policy. Describe what happened, provide photographs and give the policy number and the details of the work. Ask whether the insurer wants to appoint a loss adjuster, and whether you may instruct a conservator to stabilise the work in the meantime. Keep a record of every conversation.

THE LOSS ADJUSTER

For significant claims, the insurer will usually appoint a loss adjuster, often one with experience in fine art. The adjuster investigates the cause, assesses the damage and recommends how the claim should be settled. You may wish to appoint your own loss assessor or adviser to represent you, particularly for valuable works.

HOW CLAIMS ARE SETTLED

Fine art claims are typically settled in one of three ways.

  • Repair. The insurer pays for conservation to restore the work.
  • Repair plus depreciation. The insurer pays for conservation and for any loss of value the work has suffered despite repair. Many specialist policies include this cover; household policies often do not.
  • Total loss. If the work cannot be repaired, or repair costs exceed its value, the insurer pays the insured value and may take ownership of the damaged work.

The basis of your cover matters. An agreed-value policy pays the sum stated on the schedule, while a market-value policy pays the value at the time of loss, which may need to be established by valuation. No. 26 in this series explains the different types of valuation.

DEPRECIATION AND DOCUMENTATION

Claims for loss of value depend on evidence. A conservator's report describing the damage and the treatment, and a valuer's opinion of the work's value before and after, are usually required. Good documentation from before the accident, including condition reports, photographs and previous valuations, makes the case much stronger.

DAMAGE IN TRANSIT OR AT A THIRD PARTY'S PREMISES

If the damage occurred while the work was with a shipper, framer, gallery or conservator, notify them in writing immediately. Their insurance may respond, and their terms may set short deadlines for claims. Check the condition of works on delivery, and note any damage on the delivery receipt before signing it.

AFTER THE REPAIR

Keep the conservator's report and the claim documents with the work's records. Future buyers will want to know what happened and how it was addressed. A properly documented repair is far less concerning to a buyer than an unexplained one.
