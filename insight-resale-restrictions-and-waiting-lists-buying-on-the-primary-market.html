Resale Restrictions and Waiting Lists: Buying on the Primary Market

When a gallery sells a new work by an artist it represents, it is not simply selling to the first buyer with funds. It is placing the work, and the terms of that placement can follow the buyer for years.

No. 32  ·  Buying  ·  4 minute read

WHY GALLERIES CHOOSE THEIR BUYERS

A gallery's long-term interest lies in building an artist's reputation. Works that go to museums, serious private collections and well-regarded corporate collections support that reputation. Works that appear at auction within a year or two of leaving the studio can damage it, because a quick resale at a price above the gallery's own price invites speculation, and one below it undermines the primary market.

For artists in demand, galleries keep waiting lists. A place on the list depends on the buyer's history with the gallery, the quality of the collection the work would join, and sometimes willingness to buy works that are less sought after. This is not a hidden practice, but it is rarely written down, and new buyers are often surprised by it.

WHAT A RESALE RESTRICTION IS

Some galleries attach contractual terms to the sale of a new work. The most common are:

  • A no-resale period. The buyer agrees not to resell the work for a set time, often between two and five years.
  • A right of first refusal. If the buyer later wishes to sell, the gallery or the artist must be offered the work first, at a stated price or at the price the buyer has been offered elsewhere.
  • A restriction on auction sales. The buyer agrees not to consign the work to auction, while private resale through the gallery remains possible.
  • A buy-back clause. The gallery may repurchase the work at the original price, or at an agreed figure, if the buyer breaches the terms.

These terms are usually set out on the invoice or in a short resale agreement signed at the point of sale. Read the invoice in full before you pay it, not afterwards.

ARE THESE TERMS BINDING?

Generally, a contractual promise binds the person who made it. Whether a particular clause can be enforced depends on its wording, the law that governs the contract and the circumstances of the breach. A restriction that is reasonable in length and scope is more likely to be upheld than one that is open-ended. The practical consequences of breaching such a term are often more immediate than the legal ones: a gallery that discovers a quick resale may decline to sell to that buyer again, and word travels across the trade.

These clauses usually bind only the first buyer. They do not normally follow the work to a later owner unless that owner has also agreed to them. Even so, a buyer on the secondary market should ask whether a restriction was attached to the first sale, because a breach can lead to a dispute that touches the work itself.

QUESTIONS TO ASK BEFORE YOU AGREE

  • How long does the restriction last, and when does it start?
  • Does it cover all sales, or only sales at auction?
  • If a right of first refusal applies, at what price and within what time must the gallery respond?
  • What happens if circumstances change, for example a divorce, a death or a business sale?
  • Does the restriction pass to your heirs?
  • Is the restriction recorded on the invoice, and does the invoice name the buyer correctly?

WHY IT CAN WORK IN THE BUYER'S FAVOUR

A resale restriction is a sign that the gallery is managing the artist's market with care. For a buyer who intends to live with the work, it costs nothing. It can also protect existing owners from a sudden flood of works at auction. The restriction becomes a problem only when the buyer's real plan was a quick sale, and in that case the restriction is doing exactly what it was designed to do.

JOINING A WAITING LIST

If you want a work by an artist whose shows sell out before they open, build a relationship with the gallery. Visit exhibitions, buy works that suit your collection, ask sensible questions and be clear about what you collect and why. Galleries remember buyers who are honest about their intentions. They also remember those who ask, at the first meeting, what a work might be worth in three years.
