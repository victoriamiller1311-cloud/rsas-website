Artist Collectives and Artist-Run Spaces: Where Careers Begin

Long before an artist joins a commercial gallery, they are usually showing work in spaces run by artists themselves. For collectors who want to find talent early, these spaces are among the most rewarding places to look.

No. 44  ·  Emerging Artists  ·  4 minute read

WHAT THEY ARE

An artist-run space is a gallery, studio or project space organised and managed by artists rather than by a commercial dealer. Some occupy permanent premises; others take over empty shops, warehouses or flats for a few weeks. Collectives are groups of artists who work, exhibit or publish together, sometimes under a shared name. Both are a long-established part of the art world. Many of the most influential movements of the last century began in spaces of this kind.

WHY THEY MATTER

Artist-run spaces are where artists test ideas, take risks and build reputations among their peers. Curators, critics and gallerists visit them to see what is new. An artist who shows regularly in respected artist-run spaces is often on the radar of commercial galleries before any collector has heard of them. No. 18 in this series covers the related routes of graduate shows, residencies and prizes.

WHAT TO EXPECT AS A VISITOR

  • Opening hours can be limited, often only at weekends or by appointment.
  • Price lists may not be displayed, and some works may not be for sale at all.
  • The artists are often present and happy to talk about their work.
  • The work may be experimental, temporary or unfinished in a conventional sense. Not everything shown will be a finished object to take home.

Go with curiosity rather than a shopping list. The best visits often begin with conversation.

BUYING FROM AN ARTIST-RUN SPACE

Many works shown in artist-run spaces can be bought, either through the space or directly from the artist. Prices are usually modest compared with commercial galleries. A few practical points apply.

  • Ask who is selling. Is the space taking a commission, or will you pay the artist directly?
  • Get an invoice. Even for a small purchase, ask for a dated invoice naming the artist, title, medium, dimensions and price. It is the first document in the work's provenance.
  • Ask about the artist's other representation. If the artist already works with a gallery, they may be bound to sell through it.
  • Be fair on price. Haggling over a modest price with an artist who is paying for their own materials and space rarely builds goodwill.
  • Record the context. Keep the exhibition leaflet, invitation or a photograph of the installation. These records become part of the work's history.

READING THE SIGNALS

Not every artist-run space has the same standing. Some have long histories and alumni who are now widely known; others are new and untested. Signals worth noting include who has shown there before, whether the programme is reviewed in the art press, whether curators and gallerists attend openings, and whether the space receives public funding, which usually involves some independent assessment of quality.

As with any early-stage buying, enthusiasm should be balanced with patience. No. 04 in this series describes the warning signs of hype in an emerging artist's market, and they apply here too.

SUPPORTING THE SPACES THEMSELVES

Artist-run spaces operate on very small budgets. Many offer memberships, patrons' schemes or editions produced to raise funds. Buying an edition or joining as a supporter is a practical way to contribute, and it often brings invitations to openings and studio visits. No. 45 in this series looks at other ways collectors can support emerging artists.
