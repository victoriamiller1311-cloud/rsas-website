Condition Reports: What They Cover and When You Need One

A condition report records the physical state of a work of art. It can reveal problems a photograph will never show, and it is one of the simplest ways to protect yourself before you buy.

Buying  ·  4 minute read

When buying a work of art, most attention goes to the artist, the image and the price. The physical state of the object gets less thought than it should. Yet condition affects value, the cost of ownership and, in time, what the work will be worth when you come to sell.

A condition report is how you find out what you are actually buying.

WHAT A CONDITION REPORT IS

A condition report is a written description of the physical state of a work at a particular moment. It is usually prepared by a conservator, a specialist at an auction house or gallery, or a registrar experienced in examining works of art. It is typically accompanied by photographs, sometimes including detailed images of particular areas.

The report describes the work as it is, noting damage, wear, alteration and previous restoration. It is not a valuation and it does not confirm authenticity. Its purpose is to record condition accurately so that buyer and seller share the same understanding of the object.

WHAT A REPORT TYPICALLY COVERS

The details vary with the medium, but a thorough report on a painting will generally address the following.

The support, whether canvas, panel or board, and its stability, including tears, holes, warping, or distortion.

The ground and paint layers, noting cracking, flaking, lifting paint, losses, abrasion or discolouration.

The surface, including dirt, discoloured varnish, scratches and accretions.

Evidence of previous restoration, such as retouching, overpainting, relining or filled losses, often identified by examining the work under ultraviolet light.

The frame, the back of the work, and any labels, stamps or inscriptions, which can also be relevant to provenance.

For works on paper, a report will look closely at fading, discolouration, foxing, staining, tears, creases and the quality of the mount and frame. For photographs, it will consider fading, colour shifts, surface damage and the stability of the print. For sculpture and installation, it will cover structural integrity, surface finish, patina and any missing or replaced parts.

WHY CONDITION MATTERS SO MUCH

Condition can have a dramatic effect on value. Two works by the same artist, similar in size and date, can sell for very different prices because one is in excellent condition and the other has been damaged or heavily restored.

Some damage is minor and easily treated. Some is significant and costly to address. Some restoration is sympathetic and stable, while poor restoration can itself become a problem requiring further treatment. A report helps you understand which is which.

Condition also affects the future. A work with an unstable surface may deteriorate further, and a buyer who later wishes to sell will find that informed buyers ask the same questions.

WHEN TO ASK FOR A REPORT

For any work of meaningful value, a condition report is worth requesting before you commit.

At auction, houses will usually provide a condition report on request for the lots they offer. Ask well before the sale. These reports are typically provided as a courtesy and come with careful wording making clear that they are opinions rather than guarantees, and that the buyer should rely on their own inspection. Auction terms generally sell works as they are, so it is important to understand condition before bidding.

In a private sale or a gallery purchase, ask the seller for any existing report and consider commissioning your own. For a significant work, an independent report from a conservator you have chosen provides a second, impartial opinion.

On the primary market, new works are usually in good condition, but it is still sensible to inspect the work carefully, particularly with fragile media or works that have travelled for exhibitions.

READING A REPORT SENSIBLY

Condition reports use specialist vocabulary, and their tone can be alarming. A report listing several issues is not necessarily bad news. Very few works, even recent ones, are in perfect condition, and an honest report will mention small imperfections that have little effect on value or appearance.

The important questions are these. Are the issues stable? Do they affect the image? What would treatment involve and cost? Would treatment change the work's character? If anything in the report is unclear, ask the person who wrote it, or ask a conservator to explain.

AFTER YOU BUY

Keep the condition report with the work's other documents. It provides a baseline for insurance claims, for monitoring the work over time and for any future sale. Good care, including stable humidity and temperature, protection from direct sunlight and conservation-grade framing, will help the work stay in the condition you bought it in.
