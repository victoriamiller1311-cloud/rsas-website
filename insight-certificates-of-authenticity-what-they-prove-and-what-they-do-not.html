Certificates of Authenticity: What They Prove and What They Do Not

A certificate of authenticity is one of the first documents a buyer asks to see. It can be valuable evidence, but it is not proof in itself. Its worth depends entirely on who issued it, when, and on what basis.

Buying  ·  5 minute read

WHAT A CERTIFICATE IS

A certificate of authenticity is a written statement that a particular work was made by a named artist. For contemporary art it is usually issued by the artist, the artist's studio or the gallery that first sold the work. For an artist who has died, it may come from the estate, a foundation or the author of the catalogue raisonné, the scholarly record of the artist's output.

A good certificate identifies the work precisely. It gives the title, date, medium, dimensions, any edition number and, ideally, a photograph. It is signed and dated by someone with the standing to make the statement. Some artists add inventory numbers, embossed stamps or holograms that tie the certificate to the object.

There is no standard form in English law, and no public register of certificates. That is the first point to understand. A certificate is a private document, and anyone can produce something that looks like one.

WHAT IT CAN TELL YOU

From the right source, a certificate is strong evidence. A statement from a living artist that they made the work carries obvious weight. So does a certificate from an established gallery that represented the artist at the time the work left the studio, particularly if it matches the gallery's own sales records.

A certificate also helps on resale. Auction houses and dealers will ask for it, and a work with a clear certificate from a recognised source is usually easier to sell than one without. For some artists, particularly those who make conceptual or instruction-based work, the certificate is effectively part of the work itself, and losing it can seriously damage value.

WHAT IT CANNOT TELL YOU

A certificate does not establish ownership. It says who made the work, not who owns it now or whether the seller is entitled to sell it. Those are questions of provenance and title, which we cover in our guide to provenance.

Nor does a certificate describe condition. A genuine work may have been damaged or heavily restored since the certificate was signed. And a certificate is not a valuation. A figure written on it, if there is one, reflects a moment in time and the interests of whoever wrote it.

Most importantly, a certificate is only as reliable as its author. A certificate from a gallery with no documented link to the artist, or from a "specialist" with no recognised standing, adds little. In some fraud cases forged certificates have been used precisely because buyers treat them as a substitute for checking.

WHEN FOUNDATIONS STOP AUTHENTICATING

Buyers are sometimes surprised to find that no certificate is available for an important artist. Around 2011 and 2012, several major foundations, including those for Andy Warhol, Roy Lichtenstein, Keith Haring and Jean-Michel Basquiat, closed their authentication boards, largely because of the cost of litigation from owners whose works were rejected.

For those artists, and others like them, the market now relies on catalogues raisonnés, scholarly opinion, scientific analysis and documented provenance. The absence of a certificate is therefore not a warning sign in itself. A certificate from a body that no longer issues them, or never did, certainly is.

HOW TO CHECK A CERTIFICATE

A few practical steps will settle most questions:

  • Confirm who signed it and what authority they had to do so at the time.
  • Contact the issuer directly, using contact details you have found independently rather than those printed on the document.
  • Check that the description matches the work exactly, including dimensions, medium and any edition number.
  • Look for the work in the catalogue raisonné, if one exists, and compare the entries.
  • Ask whether the issuing gallery or studio has a record of the sale.
  • Be cautious of certificates issued long after the work was made, or by a party who stands to gain from the sale.

CERTIFICATES FOR EDITIONS AND PHOTOGRAPHS

Prints and photographs raise their own questions. A certificate should state the total edition size, the number of artist's proofs and any other impressions, and which number this example carries. If the artist made the work in several sizes, it should say which. Our guide to editions explains why these details affect price.

For digital and media works, the certificate may set out how the work is to be displayed, which files or equipment are authorised, and what happens if technology becomes obsolete. These terms matter, and they should transfer to you on purchase.

KEEPING THE PAPERWORK TOGETHER

Once you own a work, keep the original certificate with the invoice, condition report and any correspondence. Store copies separately. When you sell, the certificate should pass to the buyer, and its loss can reduce the price significantly. A certificate that has been lost cannot always be reissued, particularly if the artist has died.
