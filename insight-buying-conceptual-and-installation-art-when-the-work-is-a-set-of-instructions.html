Buying Conceptual and Installation Art: When the Work Is a Set of Instructions

Some of the most important art of the past sixty years cannot simply be hung on a wall. A wall drawing may be made by assistants following the artist's instructions. A light sculpture may use tubes that are replaced when they fail. A pile of sweets may be replenished by the owner. In each case, what the buyer acquires is not only an object, but the right to make, show or maintain the work as the artist intended.

No. 56  ·  Buying  ·  4 minute read

WHY THESE WORKS ARE DIFFERENT

From the 1960s onwards, many artists separated the idea of a work from its physical form. Sol LeWitt's wall drawings, for example, are executed from his written instructions, and can be painted out and redrawn in a new location. Dan Flavin's sculptures use commercially available fluorescent tubes, which are replaced when they fail. Félix González-Torres made works from piles of wrapped sweets that visitors may take, with the pile replenished over time.

In works like these, the physical materials are not the whole work, and sometimes not the most important part. What matters is the authority to realise the work correctly.

THE ROLE OF THE CERTIFICATE

For many conceptual and installation works, the certificate is central. It may set out:

  • What the work consists of, and its permitted dimensions or variations.
  • How it is to be installed, displayed and maintained.
  • Who may install it, and whether the artist's studio must be involved.
  • Which materials may be replaced, and how.
  • What happens if the work is sold, lent or destroyed.

Losing the certificate can be serious. In some cases a work without its certificate cannot be shown or sold as genuine. Keep certificates and instructions safe, and store copies separately. Our guide to certificates of authenticity covers the general points.

QUESTIONS BEFORE YOU BUY

  • What exactly is being sold: an object, a set of instructions, a licence, or a combination?
  • Is the certificate original, and does it pass to you on purchase?
  • Who has authority to install or re-create the work, especially after the artist's death?
  • Can the work exist in more than one place at once? Some certificates forbid it.
  • Are replacement materials still available, and who decides what is acceptable?
  • Are there conditions on resale, loan or display?

INSTALLATION AND MAINTENANCE

Installation works can need significant space, specialist installers and ongoing maintenance. Ask for written installation instructions and, ideally, photographs or video of previous installations approved by the artist or studio. Budget for installation and de-installation costs, which can be substantial.

Technical components, such as lighting, projectors or electronics, will need replacement. Some artists specify exact models, which may no longer be made. Agree in advance how obsolescence will be handled. Our guide to video and digital works discusses this for media art.

RIGHTS AND RESPONSIBILITIES

Owning a conceptual work often brings obligations as well as rights. Certificates may prevent the owner from altering the work, displaying it in certain ways or making additional versions. The artist's moral rights, discussed in our guide to copyright, may also apply. Breaching these terms can damage the work's status and value.

POINTS FOR BUYERS

  • Treat the certificate and instructions as part of the work.
  • Confirm the seller has the authority to transfer them.
  • Ask about installation history and approved installers.
  • Plan for space, maintenance and replacement parts.
  • Keep a full record of every installation.

HOW WE CAN HELP

We review certificates and documentation for conceptual and installation works, confirm their history with studios and estates where possible, and help plan installation and care.
