What a Gallery Price Tells You, and What It Does Not

A gallery price is a carefully considered number. It is not a valuation, and the difference matters to anyone spending serious money on contemporary art.

Buying  ·  7 minute read

Walk into a commercial gallery and the price of a work, if it is shown at all, appears with an air of finality. It sits on a price list beside the title, the medium and the dimensions, and it seems to settle the question of what the work is worth. For most buyers, that is where the enquiry ends.

It should be where it begins. A gallery price carries real information, and an experienced buyer can read a good deal into it. But it is also a figure set by one party to the sale, for reasons only partly connected with the work in front of you. Knowing which parts of the number you can rely on, and which you cannot, is one of the most useful habits a collector can develop.

WHO SETS THE PRICE, AND HOW

In the primary market, where a work is sold for the first time, the price is usually agreed between the artist and the gallery that represents them. The gallery then takes a commission on the sale. A split of roughly half to the artist and half to the gallery is the long-standing convention, although it is a custom rather than a rule. Terms vary with the artist's standing and with who bears the costs of production, framing and shipping.

That commission is not pure profit. It pays for the space, the staff, the exhibitions, the art fairs and the slow work of introducing an artist to collectors and curators. Fairs alone are a considerable undertaking: the Art Basel and UBS Art Market Report 2026 found that they accounted for 35 per cent of dealer sales in 2025, and a stand at a leading fair represents a substantial outlay before a single work is sold. Part of what you pay for a gallery work, in other words, is the cost of building the artist's career.

The figure itself is normally reached by convention rather than calculation. Galleries tend to price an artist's work consistently by medium and size, so that a large canvas costs more than a small one from the same series, and a work on paper less than a painting. Size is a guide, not a formula. A smaller work that is central to an artist's practice may be priced above a larger one that is not. What matters to the gallery is that its prices follow a logic it can explain, and that the same work carries the same price whether it is offered in the gallery, at a fair or online.

Prices then move in steps. A sold-out exhibition, a museum acquisition or a significant prize will often prompt an increase for the next body of work. Sensible galleries raise prices gradually, because a sudden leap can price out the very collectors who supported the artist early on.

WHAT THE PRICE TELLS YOU

Read properly, a gallery price tells you several useful things.

It tells you what the artist and the gallery believe the market will currently bear for new work of that size and medium. That belief is usually grounded in experience: how quickly the last exhibition sold, how many enquiries came in, whether there is a waiting list.

It also tells you something about the gallery's commitment. Primary market prices very rarely fall. Galleries guard them closely, because a public reduction undermines every collector who has already bought and signals weakness to everyone watching. A gallery that has held or raised an artist's prices across several exhibitions is, at the very least, confident in the work.

Most revealing of all is the trajectory. An artist whose prices have risen steadily, in modest increments, alongside exhibitions and institutional interest, has a market built on something. An artist whose prices have doubled within a year deserves closer questions.

WHAT IT DOES NOT TELL YOU

This is the more important half.

A gallery price is not a valuation. No independent party has tested it, and it has not been set by competitive bidding. It is the price at which the gallery is prepared to sell, which is a different thing from the price at which you could later sell.

Nor does it tell you what other buyers paid. Discounts are a routine part of the primary market. Established collectors, museums and those buying several works are commonly offered a reduction, often in the region of ten per cent and sometimes more for institutions. The listed price and the price actually achieved can therefore differ, and there is rarely any public record of the gap.

It does not tell you how deep the demand runs. A gallery can sell out a show to a small circle of loyal clients, and from outside the result looks identical to a show that sold to a broad and growing base of collectors. The first is far more fragile than the second.

It does not tell you whether the price will hold on resale. Once a work is sold again, at auction or privately, the gallery no longer sets the number. The secondary market shows what buyers will pay when nobody is managing the price, and for many younger artists there is no resale history at all. That absence is not necessarily a bad sign, but it does mean the gallery price is untested. Where resale results exist, they may sit above the gallery price or well below it. Selling also carries costs of its own. At auction the seller pays a commission and the buyer pays a premium on top of the hammer price, so a work generally needs to achieve noticeably more than you paid before you come out level.

Finally, a gallery price does not tell you whether you can buy the work at all. For artists in demand, galleries choose their buyers. They favour collectors with a record of keeping work, and those likely to lend or give to museums. The price on the list may in practice be available only to people the gallery already knows.

THE TERMS THAT COME WITH THE PRICE

A purchase from a gallery can also carry conditions that are easy to overlook. Resale restrictions have become far more common in recent years, particularly for artists whose work has attracted speculation. Typically the buyer agrees not to resell for a set period, often three to five years, and to offer the work back to the gallery first should they decide to sell.

These clauses have teeth. In one widely reported case, a collector bought a painting from a New York gallery at Art Basel Miami Beach in 2019 for $700,000, and the work was resold within months. When the gallery learned of the resale it threatened legal action under its terms of sale, and the matter was settled privately. How far such clauses can be enforced against private buyers, particularly under English consumer law, has yet to be decided by the courts. Galleries rarely need a judgment to make their displeasure felt, however. A buyer who breaks a resale agreement may simply find that the next work is offered to someone else.

None of this is a reason not to buy. It is a reason to read the invoice and the terms of sale before you pay, and to ask about any condition you do not understand.

TWO MARKETS, READ TOGETHER

The clearest picture of an artist's market usually comes from comparing the two. If gallery prices have risen steadily and resale results have kept pace or exceeded them, the gallery's pricing has been confirmed by buyers acting independently. If gallery prices have climbed while the few works that reached auction failed to sell or sold well below estimate, the primary market may be running ahead of genuine demand. And if there is no resale record at all, the honest conclusion is that the market has not yet been tested.

This is not to diminish the role of galleries. They remain the heart of the contemporary trade. According to the same Art Basel and UBS report, dealers accounted for an estimated $34.8 billion of the $59.6 billion in global art sales in 2025. Most collectors will buy most of their contemporary art through galleries, and the best of them price fairly and with great care. The point is not to distrust the price. It is to understand what kind of number it is.

QUESTIONS WORTH ASKING BEFORE YOU BUY

■ How have this artist's prices changed over the last three to five years, and what prompted each increase? ■ Is the work offered at the same price elsewhere, at a fair or online? ■ Is a discount available, and on what basis? ■ Are there resale restrictions, a right of first refusal or any other conditions in the terms of sale? ■ Has the artist's work appeared at auction, and if so, how did it perform against estimate? ■ How widely have recent exhibitions sold, and has the gallery placed work with museums or public collections?

A good gallery will answer most of these readily. Where the answers are thin or evasive, that is information too.
