Selling Inherited Art: A Guide for Executors and Families

Inherited art often arrives at a difficult time. Executors and families must decide what to keep, what to sell and how, often while dealing with grief, deadlines and differing views. Careful steps early on protect both the value of the works and the relationships around them.

Selling  ·  5 minute read

This guide reflects the position in England and Wales at the time of writing. It is general information and not legal or tax advice. Estates with significant art holdings should take professional advice.

FIRST, SECURE THE WORKS

Before any decision about selling, make sure the works are safe. Check that insurance remains in force, as cover can lapse or change when a policyholder dies. Keep the works where they are unless there is a reason to move them, and avoid informal distribution among family members before the estate has been valued and the will interpreted.

FIND OUT WHAT THERE IS

Make an inventory. For each work, record the artist, title, medium, size, location and any labels or inscriptions, and take clear photographs of the front and back. Gather whatever paperwork exists: invoices, certificates, insurance schedules, correspondence with galleries and earlier valuations.

Check whether any works are on loan to a museum or exhibition, held in storage, consigned to a dealer or used as security for a loan. Works that are physically elsewhere are easily overlooked. Equally, check whether anything in the house belongs to someone else.

THE PROBATE VALUATION

For inheritance tax, the estate must be valued as at the date of death. Art is valued at open market value, meaning the price it might reasonably be expected to fetch if sold on the open market at that time. This is usually lower than an insurance value, which reflects the cost of replacement.

The valuation should be prepared by a qualified, independent valuer and should be defensible. HM Revenue and Customs can challenge values it considers too low, and penalties can apply where a valuation is careless. A value set too high, on the other hand, can mean paying more tax than necessary. Our guide to valuations explains the different types in more detail.

INHERITANCE TAX IN OUTLINE

Art forms part of the estate like any other asset. Inheritance tax is generally charged at 40 per cent on the value of the estate above the available nil-rate band, currently £325,000 per person, with additional reliefs in some circumstances. Assets left to a surviving spouse or civil partner, or to charity, are generally exempt.

Two schemes deserve mention for important works. Conditional exemption can defer tax on works of national importance in return for undertakings about care and public access. Acceptance in Lieu allows important works to be offered to the nation in settlement of tax. Both are specialist routes with strict conditions.

Where the deceased lived or held assets outside the UK, other countries' inheritance and succession rules may also apply. Spain, for example, has its own inheritance tax system.

CAPITAL GAINS TAX AFTER DEATH

Beneficiaries generally inherit works at their probate value, which becomes the starting point for capital gains tax on any later sale. If a work is sold for more than its probate value, the gain may be taxable. If it is sold for less, the loss may be relievable in some circumstances. This is one reason the probate valuation matters for later decisions as well as for inheritance tax.

KEEPING, DIVIDING OR SELLING

The will may leave particular works to named people. Otherwise, executors must decide with beneficiaries whether works should be divided, sold or kept. Disagreements are common, particularly where one work is worth much more than others, or where family members attach different sentimental value to the same piece.

Some families agree to sell everything and divide the proceeds. Others take turns choosing, with values balanced in cash. Whatever the approach, agreeing it in writing early avoids misunderstanding later.

CHOOSING HOW TO SELL

Executors have a duty to act in the estate's interest, which usually means seeking a fair price rather than the quickest sale. That said, they also need to administer the estate within a reasonable time.

The main routes are auction, private sale and consignment to a dealer, each with different costs, timing and visibility. Our guide to selling sets these out in detail. Estates are sometimes approached by buyers offering to clear the contents quickly. Such offers are rarely the best price for anything of value, and should be compared with independent advice.

AVOIDING COMMON MISTAKES

  • Selling before the probate valuation is complete.
  • Throwing away papers, labels or old photographs, which can be important to provenance.
  • Cleaning or restoring works before taking advice.
  • Accepting the first offer, particularly from someone who has also valued the work.
  • Overlooking the Artist's Resale Right, which may apply to sales of work by living artists or those who died within the last 70 years.

HOW WE CAN HELP

We work with executors, solicitors and families to identify works, gather documentation, research markets and recommend the right route to sale. We do not prepare probate valuations or give tax advice, and we can introduce independent valuers and advisers where needed.

If you are responsible for an estate that includes art, speak to an adviser before making any decision to sell.
