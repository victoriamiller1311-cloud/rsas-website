When a Purchase Goes Wrong: Mediation, Arbitration and the Courts

Most art transactions go smoothly. When they do not, the dispute may concern authenticity, ownership, condition, payment or the terms of a consignment. Knowing the routes available, and their costs, helps buyers and sellers choose the right one and act in time.

No. 67  ·  Research  ·  4 minute read

This guide outlines the position in England and Wales. It is general information, not legal advice, and anyone facing a significant dispute should consult a solicitor promptly.

THE COMMON DISPUTES

  • *Authenticity.* A work turns out not to be by the artist it was sold as.
  • *Title.* A work proves to have been stolen, looted or sold by someone without the right to sell it.
  • *Description and condition.* A work does not match what was described, or has undisclosed damage or restoration.
  • *Payment.* A buyer does not pay, or a seller does not pass on the proceeds of a sale.
  • *Consignment.* A dealer or auction house fails to return a work, sells it below the agreed price, or does not account for the sale.

START WITH THE PAPERWORK

The first step is always the documents: the invoice, the terms of sale, the catalogue description, any certificate or condition report, and the correspondence. Auction houses' terms often contain specific remedies, such as a right to return a work found to be a forgery within a set period. Our guides to certificates and provenance explain what those documents should contain.

NEGOTIATION

Many disputes are resolved by direct discussion. Reputable dealers and auction houses have an interest in their reputation and will often take back a work or refund the price when a genuine problem is shown. Put your concerns in writing, set out the facts clearly, and keep copies of everything.

MEDIATION

Mediation brings in a neutral third party to help the parties reach their own agreement. It is confidential, relatively quick and much cheaper than litigation, and it allows creative solutions, such as a partial refund or the return of a work in exchange for another. The courts in England and Wales strongly encourage alternative dispute resolution, and the Court of Appeal confirmed in 2023 that courts can order parties to engage in it.

ARBITRATION

Arbitration is a private process in which an arbitrator, or panel, decides the dispute and makes a binding award. It suits parties who want expertise and confidentiality. The Court of Arbitration for Art, established in The Hague in 2018 by the Netherlands Arbitration Institute and Authentication in Art, offers arbitration and mediation by specialists in art disputes, with tribunal-appointed experts for questions of authenticity and scientific analysis. Arbitration usually requires both parties to agree to it, ideally in the original contract.

THE COURTS

Litigation remains the route of last resort. Courts can order the return of a work, damages or a refund, and their decisions are public and enforceable. They are also expensive, slow and uncertain, particularly on questions of authenticity, where courts have often been reluctant to decide who painted a work.

TIME LIMITS

Time limits matter. In England and Wales, most claims in contract must be brought within six years, although fraud or deliberate concealment can extend the period. Claims involving stolen works follow special rules. Do not delay once you suspect a problem.

PREVENTING DISPUTES

Most disputes can be prevented by careful buying: clear contracts, written descriptions, independent condition reports, proper provenance research and clauses that say how disputes will be resolved. Our article on consignment agreements covers the selling side.

HOW WE CAN HELP

We research the facts behind a dispute, including provenance, attribution history and comparable sales, and work alongside your solicitor. We do not give legal advice.

If you have concerns about a purchase or sale, speak to an adviser.
