Provenance: The Questions to Ask Before You Buy

Provenance is the documented history of a work of art. It protects you against fakes, stolen goods and disputed ownership, and it affects what the work will be worth when you come to sell.

Buying  ·  7 minute read

Every work of art has a history. It was made by someone, at a certain time, and has passed through a series of hands to reach the person now offering it to you. Provenance is the record of that journey: who made the work, who has owned it, where it has been shown and how it changed hands.

A complete provenance does three things for a buyer. It supports the attribution, because a work that can be traced back to the artist's studio is far less likely to be a fake. It supports the title, because it shows that each owner was entitled to sell. And it supports value, because a clean and well-documented history makes a work easier to insure, to lend and to resell. A gap in that history does not mean something is wrong. It does mean there is a question that has not yet been answered.

WHY IT MATTERS EVEN FOR NEW WORK

It is tempting to think provenance is a concern only for Old Masters and works that changed hands during the Second World War. For contemporary art, the history is usually short and often simple: the work left the studio, was sold by the artist's gallery, and may have passed to one or two later owners. That simplicity is an advantage, because the record should be easy to complete. When it is not, the gap stands out.

The art market has learned the cost of skipping these checks. Between the mid-1990s and 2008, a respected New York gallery and another dealer sold dozens of paintings attributed to leading Abstract Expressionists, for around $80 million in total. All were forgeries, supplied with an invented history involving an anonymous private collector. The gallery, which had traded for more than 160 years, closed in 2011, and the case led to years of litigation.

More recent cases have turned on title rather than authenticity. In 2022 a London-based dealer in contemporary art was sentenced in New York to seven years in prison for an $86 million fraud. Among other things, he had sold shares in the same works to different investors, in some cases more than 100 per cent of a single painting, and used works as security for loans without disclosing it. Buyers who believed they owned those works found that others had a claim to them too.

THE QUESTIONS TO ASK

The following questions apply to any significant purchase, from a gallery, a dealer, an auction house or a private seller. A reputable seller will expect them.

1. CAN THE HISTORY BE TRACED BACK TO THE ARTIST?

Ask for the full chain of ownership, from the studio to the present. For a recent work, that may simply be the original gallery invoice and a record of any later sale. For an older one, it may involve exhibition catalogues, auction records, letters and photographs. Look for documents that were created at the time, rather than descriptions written later. A provenance that relies on phrases such as "private collection, Europe" may be perfectly innocent, since many owners value their privacy, but it cannot be checked, and it should be confirmed in confidence where possible.

2. WHO HAS CONFIRMED THAT THE WORK IS GENUINE?

For a living artist, the most reliable confirmation usually comes from the artist's studio or their representing gallery. For artists who have died, it may come from the artist's estate, a foundation or the author of the catalogue raisonné, the scholarly record of the artist's known works. Bear in mind that several foundations have stopped issuing opinions on authenticity because of the legal risks involved, so the absence of a certificate is not always a warning sign in itself.

Certificates of authenticity should be treated with care. A certificate is only as reliable as the person who signed it, and certificates themselves can be forged. Ask who issued it, and whether that person or body can confirm it independently.

3. IS THE SELLER ENTITLED TO SELL?

Under English law, a thief cannot pass good title. A buyer who acquires a stolen work, even in complete good faith, can be required to return it to the rightful owner, subject to the relevant limitation periods. The old rule of "market overt", which once protected some purchases made in open markets, was abolished in 1995.

Title can also be clouded without any theft. A work may be subject to a loan, used as security for borrowing, owned jointly, held under a consignment agreement that limits what the dealer can do, or caught up in a divorce, a bankruptcy or a dispute between heirs. Ask the seller to confirm in writing that they own the work outright, or are authorised by the owner to sell it, and that it is free of any charge, lien or claim.

4. HAS THE WORK BEEN CHECKED AGAINST STOLEN ART DATABASES?

The two best-known resources are the Art Loss Register, a private database of some 700,000 items reported as stolen, missing or looted, or subject to disputes over title, and Interpol's Stolen Works of Art database, which holds almost 57,000 items and can be searched through its ID-Art app. Auction houses and many dealers search these routinely before sale. Ask whether a search has been carried out, and for a copy of the certificate where one was issued. A clear search is a useful safeguard rather than a guarantee, because a work can only appear if its loss has been reported.

5. ARE THERE ANY RESALE RESTRICTIONS OR OTHER CONDITIONS?

Many works bought from galleries in recent years carry contractual terms, such as an agreement not to resell for a set period or to offer the work back to the gallery first. If you are buying on the secondary market, ask whether any such terms were attached to the original sale and whether they still apply. Our piece on gallery pricing explains how these clauses work.

6. WHAT DO THE PHYSICAL DETAILS TELL YOU?

A work's own surface often carries part of its history. Labels from galleries, exhibitions and shippers on the back of a canvas or frame, inventory numbers, stamps and inscriptions can all be checked against the documents. For editions, such as prints, photographs and cast sculpture, confirm the edition size, the number of the example you are buying and whether artist's proofs exist, since these affect both scarcity and value. A condition report from a qualified conservator will record the state of the work and any past restoration.

7. HAS THE WORK BEEN ANYWHERE THAT RAISES FURTHER QUESTIONS?

For older works, any change of ownership in Europe between 1933 and 1945 deserves particular attention, because of the scale of Nazi-era looting and forced sales. The Washington Conference Principles of 1998, endorsed by more than 40 governments, set out how such claims should be approached, and in the UK the Spoliation Advisory Panel has considered claims against public collections since 2000. Works that have passed through areas of conflict, or that include protected materials, may raise other legal issues and should be reviewed with specialist advice.

8. WILL THE PAPERWORK TRANSFER TO YOU?

Provenance has value only if it stays with the work. Make sure every document, certificate, condition report and invoice is handed over on completion and kept safely. Your own purchase invoice, naming the seller, the work and the price paid, becomes the next link in the chain for whoever owns the work after you.

PROVENANCE AND ANTI-MONEY LAUNDERING CHECKS

Questions about the history of a work increasingly sit alongside questions about the people involved. Since January 2020, businesses in the UK that trade in or act as intermediaries in the sale of works of art for €10,000 or more have been required to register with HM Revenue and Customs and carry out due diligence on their customers under the money laundering regulations. A seller who asks for proof of your identity is not being difficult. They are meeting a legal obligation, and the same scrutiny applied to the work itself is part of what protects you as a buyer.

WHEN THE ANSWERS ARE INCOMPLETE

Many perfectly genuine works have imperfect histories. Records are lost, owners die without leaving papers, and some sellers simply never kept invoices. An incomplete provenance is not a reason to walk away automatically. It is a reason to understand exactly what is missing, to weigh how much it matters, and to reflect it in the price you are prepared to pay. The one response that is never sensible is to proceed without asking.
