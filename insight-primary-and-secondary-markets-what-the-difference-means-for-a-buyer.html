Primary and Secondary Markets: What the Difference Means for a Buyer

The same artist can be sold in two very different markets, with different prices, different rules and different risks. Knowing which one you are buying in changes the questions you should ask.

Research  ·  4 minute read

The art market is often spoken about as though it were a single place. In practice it divides into two, and the division matters to anyone buying contemporary work.

The primary market is where a work is sold for the first time, usually by the artist's gallery. The secondary market is everything that follows: resale at auction, through dealers or privately between collectors. A work can pass through the secondary market many times over its life. It can only ever have one primary sale.

HOW THE PRIMARY MARKET WORKS

When a gallery represents an artist, it typically takes new work on consignment, exhibits it and sells it, sharing the proceeds with the artist. The gallery sets the price, usually in consultation with the artist, and it tends to keep prices consistent across a body of work according to size and medium.

Primary prices are not set by open competition. They are managed. A good gallery raises prices gradually as demand, critical attention and the artist's exhibition record grow. It avoids rises so steep that collectors begin to doubt them, and it rarely lowers them, since a visible reduction can damage confidence across the whole market.

Galleries also choose their buyers. For an artist in demand, new work may be offered first to collectors with a record of keeping work long term, or to those likely to lend to or donate to institutions. Some galleries ask buyers to agree not to resell within a set period, or to offer the work back to the gallery first. These arrangements are designed to keep the market orderly and to discourage quick resale for profit.

HOW THE SECONDARY MARKET WORKS

Once a work has left the gallery, its owner can sell it as they wish. Price is no longer managed by anyone in particular. At auction it is set by competition between bidders on the day. In a private sale it is negotiated.

This makes secondary prices more volatile, in both directions. A work by an artist with a long waiting list may sell at auction for well above the gallery price, because buyers unable to obtain work from the gallery are willing to pay more. Equally, a work by an artist whose market has cooled may sell well below what the original buyer paid, or not sell at all.

Secondary sales also carry costs that primary sales usually do not. Auction houses charge a buyer's premium and a seller's commission, and the Artist's Resale Right may apply to resales of work by living or recently deceased artists. These costs affect what a seller actually receives and what a buyer actually pays.

WHY THE TWO PRICES CAN DIFFER

When primary and secondary prices diverge, the gap is informative.

If secondary prices sit well above primary prices, demand is outstripping the supply the gallery is willing to release. That can be a sign of a healthy and growing market. It can also attract speculative buyers, whose resales may later unsettle the very prices that drew them in.

If secondary prices sit below primary prices, the market is telling the gallery its prices are higher than buyers will pay in open competition. Galleries sometimes respond by slowing price rises or pausing them. For a buyer, a persistent gap of this kind is a reason for caution at the primary level.

Where the two broadly agree, the market is more likely to be in balance.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR BUYERS

Buying on the primary market has real advantages. You acquire the work directly from the artist's representative, with clear provenance from the outset. Prices are generally consistent and you are supporting the artist's career directly. The trade-off is limited choice and, for sought-after artists, limited access.

Buying on the secondary market offers wider choice, including earlier works no longer available from the gallery, and sometimes the chance to buy below the current primary price. It also brings more risk. Provenance and condition need checking with greater care. Costs are higher, and the price you pay is a single result on a single day rather than a figure supported by a managed pricing structure.

In both cases the essential question is the same: what evidence supports this price? The answer simply comes from different places.

QUESTIONS TO ASK IN EITHER MARKET

On the primary market, it is worth asking how the artist's prices have moved in recent years, whether the gallery attaches any resale conditions, and how long the gallery has represented the artist.

On the secondary market, ask how the price compares with recent auction results for similar works, what the provenance is back to the first sale, and what the total cost will be once premiums, taxes and any resale royalty are added.
