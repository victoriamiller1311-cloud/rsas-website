Owning a Work Is Not Owning the Copyright

When you buy a work of art, you buy the physical object. The copyright in the image usually stays with the artist. Most collectors never need to think about the difference, until they want to print it, post it, or put it in a brochure.

Buying  ·  4 minute read

This guide summarises the position under UK law. It is general information, not legal advice.

TWO SEPARATE THINGS

UK copyright law, set out mainly in the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, treats the physical work and the copyright in it as separate property. Selling one does not transfer the other. A transfer of copyright must be made in writing and signed by or on behalf of the owner. Without that, the artist keeps the copyright, even after the work has been sold.

Copyright in an artistic work generally lasts for the artist's lifetime plus 70 years from the end of the year in which they die. After the artist's death, the copyright passes under their will or to their heirs, and is often managed by an estate, a foundation or a collecting society.

WHAT YOU CAN DO AS OWNER

Owning the work gives you a great deal of freedom. You can display it in your home or office, lend it to an exhibition, and sell or give it away. You can photograph it for your own records, insurance and condition monitoring.

The law also contains specific exceptions. One allows a work to be reproduced to advertise its sale, which is why auction houses and dealers can illustrate works they are selling. Another covers incidental inclusion, where a work appears in the background of a photograph or film without being its subject.

WHAT NEEDS PERMISSION

Reproducing the work for wider use is a different matter. Without the copyright owner's permission, you should not normally:

  • Print the image on cards, posters, books or merchandise.
  • Use it in advertising, marketing or a company report.
  • Publish it prominently on a website or social media account, other than in connection with a sale.
  • Make copies or adaptations of the work.

For businesses, this matters more than it may seem. A hotel that uses photographs of its lobby artwork in a brochure, or a company that features a painting from its collection on its website, may need a licence. Our guides to art for hotels and to briefing an art consultant touch on this from the corporate side.

GETTING PERMISSION

Permission is usually straightforward to obtain. Living artists are often happy to grant it, sometimes for a fee, and their gallery can usually help. For many artists, rights are managed by a collecting society. In the UK, DACS licenses reproductions on behalf of a large number of artists and estates, and can quote a fee for a given use.

Any licence should be in writing and should say what use is permitted, in what media, for how long and in which territories. Keep it with the work's other documents.

MORAL RIGHTS

Artists also have moral rights, which protect their reputation and connection to the work. These cannot be sold, although they can be waived in writing. The main moral rights are:

  • The right to be identified as the author when the work is exhibited in public or published, provided the artist has asserted this right.
  • The right to object to derogatory treatment of the work, meaning an addition, deletion, alteration or adaptation that is prejudicial to the artist's honour or reputation.
  • The right not to have a work falsely attributed to them.

For collectors, the practical point is simple. Altering a work, cutting it down, painting over part of it or displaying it in a way that distorts it may infringe the artist's moral rights, as well as reducing its value. Removing a signature or label, or presenting the work as by someone else, raises similar issues.

COMMISSIONS

If you commission a work, the same rule applies. Paying for the work does not give you the copyright unless the contract assigns it in writing. If you will need to reproduce a commissioned work, for marketing, publications or merchandise, agree a licence or assignment as part of the commission. Our guide to commissioning a living artist covers this in more detail.

WORKS OUTSIDE THE UK

Copyright rules differ between countries, although most follow broadly similar principles under international treaties. If you intend to use an image internationally, or the artist is based abroad, check the position in each relevant country.
