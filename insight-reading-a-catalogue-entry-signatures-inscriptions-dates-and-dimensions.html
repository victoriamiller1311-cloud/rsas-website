Reading a Catalogue Entry: Signatures, Inscriptions, Dates and Dimensions

Every auction catalogue entry and every good dealer description follows a code. The words used to describe the artist, the signature and the date are chosen with care, and small differences in wording can mean large differences in what the seller is prepared to stand behind.

No. 40  ·  Research  ·  4 minute read

THE HEADING

The first line of an entry usually names the artist. The way it is written matters.

  • The artist's full name, with no qualification, means the seller believes the work is by that artist.
  • "Attributed to" means the work is thought to be by the artist, but with less certainty.
  • "Studio of" or "workshop of" means the work was made in the artist's studio, possibly under their supervision, but not necessarily by their hand.
  • "Circle of" means the work is by an unidentified hand closely associated with the artist.
  • "Follower of" or "style of" means the work is in the artist's manner, by someone else, possibly later.
  • "After" means the work is a copy of a known work by the artist.

Each house sets out its own glossary in the conditions of sale, and the definitions are not identical. Read the glossary for the sale you are bidding in. Importantly, the guarantee of authorship offered by most major houses applies only to the heading in its unqualified form.

SIGNATURES AND INSCRIPTIONS

The description of a signature follows similar conventions.

  • "Signed" means the seller believes the signature is the artist's.
  • "Bears signature" or "inscribed with signature" means there is a signature, but the seller does not vouch that the artist wrote it.
  • "Dated" means the seller believes the date was written by the artist.
  • "Bears date" means a date is present, but may have been added by someone else.
  • "Inscribed" usually refers to other writing, such as a title or dedication, and may say whether it is in the artist's hand.

Many genuine works are unsigned. Many false works carry convincing signatures. A signature is evidence, not proof, and it should always be considered alongside provenance and the work itself.

DATE

A date given alone, such as "1987", means the work was made in that year. "Executed in 1987" often indicates the date is known from records rather than from the work. "Circa 1987" means around that time. Pay attention to the difference between the date a work was made and the date it was printed or cast, which matters greatly for photographs, prints and sculpture. No. 34 in this series explains why.

MEDIUM AND SUPPORT

The medium describes what the work is made of, and the support describes what it is made on. "Oil on canvas" is clear. "Mixed media" may cover anything from collage to paint and found objects, and is worth asking about. For prints, the technique, such as screenprint, etching or lithograph, determines how the work was made and how it should be priced. A "giclée" or "archival pigment print" is a high-quality inkjet print and is not the same as a hand-pulled print.

DIMENSIONS

Dimensions are usually given as height before width, then depth for objects, in centimetres and often also in inches. Check whether the measurement is of the image, the sheet or the frame. A work that appears smaller in person than you expected has often been measured with its frame. Comparing dimensions against a catalogue raisonné or an earlier sale is one of the simplest ways to confirm that two records refer to the same work.

PROVENANCE, EXHIBITIONS AND LITERATURE

Below the main description, most entries list the work's provenance, its exhibition history and the books or articles in which it has been reproduced. Each line is a claim that can be checked. Gaps, vague phrases such as "private collection, Europe", and missing dates deserve a question. No. 05 in this series sets out the questions to ask about provenance.

THE CONDITION REPORT IS SEPARATE

Catalogue entries rarely describe condition. Most houses provide condition reports on request and state that the absence of a report does not mean the work is in perfect condition. No. 19 explains what a condition report covers.
