Collecting Video, Light and Digital Works: What You Actually Own

A painting is a physical object that you can hang on a wall. A video installation, a light work or a digital piece is something else: a set of rights, files, equipment and instructions that together allow the work to exist. Buying one well depends on knowing exactly which of those you are getting.

No. 35  ·  Buying  ·  4 minute read

WHAT YOU ARE BUYING

When you buy a time-based or digital work, you are usually buying:

  • A certificate confirming the work's title, edition, duration and the artist's authorisation.
  • The media, such as video files, software or a master copy on a drive, sometimes with a backup.
  • Installation instructions that set out how the work must be shown: screen size, projection, sound, room conditions, lighting levels and loop settings.
  • A licence to display the work, with any limits on where and how often.
  • Sometimes, the equipment itself, such as a specific monitor, projector or neon tube.

The certificate and the instructions are often worth more than the hardware. Without them, the files alone may be little more than a copy.

EDITIONS AND EXHIBITION COPIES

Video and digital works are usually issued in editions, like prints or photographs. The edition number defines your copy, and the artist commits not to issue more. Some artists also keep exhibition copies for loans. Ask how many copies exist in total, who holds them, and whether you may make your own backup.

OBSOLESCENCE

Technology changes faster than art does. Formats become unreadable, monitors are discontinued, software stops running on new systems, and neon and fluorescent tubes burn out. The question for a buyer is what the artist allows when that happens.

  • Migration. Can the files be transferred to a new format without the artist's further consent?
  • Replacement equipment. Is the specified monitor or projector essential to the work, or can an equivalent be used?
  • Re-fabrication. For neon and light works, can broken elements be remade, and by whom?
  • Artist's estate. Who will answer these questions after the artist's death?

Good galleries and artists address these points in writing at the point of sale. If they are silent, ask. A work that cannot be shown in twenty years is a work with a limited future.

DISPLAY AND CARE

Light and screen-based works generate heat and use power. They need ventilation, safe electrical installation and sometimes acoustic consideration. Projections need controlled ambient light. Keep a written record of the settings agreed with the artist or gallery, so that anyone installing the work later can reproduce them.

Store files in at least two places, one of them off site, and check them periodically. Keep the original drive or master copy untouched and work from duplicates.

DIGITAL WORKS SOLD ON BLOCKCHAINS

Some digital works are sold with a token recorded on a blockchain. The token records a transaction, but it does not by itself transfer copyright or guarantee that the image will remain accessible. Read the terms that accompany the token to see what rights are granted, where the file is stored, and what happens if the platform closes. The same questions apply as for any other digital work: what you own, what you may do with it, and how it will be preserved.

COPYRIGHT AND USE

As No. 24 in this series explains, owning a work does not mean owning its copyright. That is especially relevant for video and digital works, which are easy to copy. Your licence will usually prohibit public screening for a fee, broadcasting and online posting of the full work, while allowing private display.

INSURANCE

Ask your insurer whether the policy covers the work as an artwork or the equipment as household goods. A specialist fine art policy should cover the work itself, including re-fabrication costs where the artist permits them.
