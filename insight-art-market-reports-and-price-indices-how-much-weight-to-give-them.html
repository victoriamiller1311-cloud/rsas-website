Art Market Reports and Price Indices: How Much Weight to Give Them

Annual market reports and art price indices are widely quoted and rarely read closely. They describe the market as a whole. They say much less about any single artist or work.

Research  ·  4 minute read

Every year brings a familiar set of headlines. Global art sales rose or fell by a certain percentage. One segment outperformed another. An index of art prices beat, or lagged behind, the stock market.

These figures are useful, provided you understand what they measure. Too often they are quoted as though they described the prospects of a particular work, which they do not.

WHAT THE ANNUAL REPORTS MEASURE

The best known annual surveys estimate total sales across the art market, broken down by region, by sales channel and by price band. They draw on auction data, which is public, and on surveys of dealers, whose sales mostly are not.

That mixture is worth noting. Auction figures are reasonably complete. Dealer figures rely on what galleries choose to report, and private sales between collectors are largely invisible. Any total for the whole market is therefore partly an estimate, however carefully it has been compiled.

The reports are valuable for understanding broad conditions. They show whether activity is concentrating in particular cities, how the share of online sales is changing, and whether the top end of the market is behaving differently from the middle. They are much less useful for judging the price of a work by an emerging artist, which may fall into a segment the headline figures barely capture.

THE WEIGHT OF THE TOP END

Total sales figures are dominated by a small number of very expensive works. A handful of exceptional auction results can lift an annual total noticeably, while thousands of modest sales barely move it.

So a reported rise in global sales does not mean that most works rose in value, or that most dealers had a good year. It may simply mean a few major collections came to auction. The reverse is also true. A fall in the headline total can sit alongside healthy activity in the lower and middle price bands where most collectors buy.

HOW ART PRICE INDICES ARE BUILT

Price indices try to answer a different question: how have prices for art changed over time?

This is harder than it sounds. Unlike shares, no two works of art are identical, and most change hands rarely. Indices deal with this in two main ways.

Repeat-sales indices track works that have sold more than once at auction and measure the change in price between sales. The method compares like with like, which is a strength. Its weakness is that only a small and unusual subset of works ever sells repeatedly at auction, and works that have fallen in value are less likely to be offered for sale at all, which can flatter the results.

Other indices use statistical models that adjust each sale for characteristics such as artist, size, medium and date. These capture far more transactions, but their results depend heavily on the assumptions built into the model.

COMMON DISTORTIONS TO KEEP IN MIND

Several features of art data tend to make indices look better than the experience of a typical owner.

Survivorship is the first. Artists whose markets collapse tend to disappear from the data, while successful artists remain. An index built on the survivors can overstate the returns available to someone choosing artists at the outset.

Unsold works are the second. When a lot fails to sell at auction, there is no price to record, so the failure may not show up in the index at all.

Costs are the third. Indices generally compare hammer prices, while owners pay buyer's premiums, insurance, storage and, on sale, a seller's commission. A work that appears to have risen in value on paper may have produced far less once these are deducted.

USING THE DATA SENSIBLY

None of this makes market reports or indices worthless. They provide context, and context matters. A buyer who knows that the market for mid-priced contemporary work has been subdued, or that a particular region is drawing new collectors, is better placed than one who does not.

The mistake is to move from the general to the particular. A rising index does not mean a given artist's prices will rise, and a falling one does not mean they will fall. Each artist's market has its own drivers: representation, critical attention, supply and the behaviour of a fairly small group of buyers.

That is why our own research focuses on the specific. We use the broad reports to understand conditions, then build our view of an individual artist from their exhibition record, primary pricing and secondary results.

A final word of caution. Comparisons between art indices and financial markets are often used to present art as an investment. Works of art are not regulated investments, they are not covered by any compensation scheme, and they can be difficult to sell. Headline index figures should never be the reason to buy.
